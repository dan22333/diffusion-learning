0The one picture that unifies all of it

Every method here reverses the same process: one that gradually turns data into noise. They differ only in what the network predicts and which equation you integrate to go back. Hold these three facts and the rest is bookkeeping.

Fact 1 — the forward process is fixed and closed‑form

Take data \(x_0\), add Gaussian noise so that at "time" \(t\):

$$x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).$$

At \(t{=}0\) it's data; at \(t{=}T\), \(\bar\alpha_t\to0\) so \(x_T\) is pure noise. No network yet — this is a definition.

Fact 2 — everything the network could predict is the same information

Given \(x_t\), these are all linear reparametrizations of one another:

$$\underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise (DDPM)}}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \underbrace{x_0}_{\text{clean image}}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \underbrace{\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)}_{\text{score}}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \underbrace{u_t}_{\text{velocity (flow)}}.$$

The bridge you'll use constantly:

$$\boxed{\;\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)=-\,\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\;}$$

So a DDPM noise‑predictor is a score network up to a known scale. This is why "score matching" and "DDPM" are secretly the same training run — and why guidance (which edits the score) applies to all of them.

Fact 3 — two legal ways to go backward

There is a stochastic reverse (an SDE — DDPM, Langevin) and a deterministic one (an ODE — DDIM, flow, EDM's ODE) that pass through the same sequence of distributions \(p_t\). Choosing one is a sampler decision, not a model change.

1DDPM — the baseline

DDPM = fix a process that turns data into noise, then learn to undo it one small step at a time. This section is the entire story, in order, nothing skipped: A the three distributions → the forward process → B the one idea (use ① to reach ②) → C training → D is the step even Gaussian? → E its mean (exact) and variance (\(\tilde\beta_t\)) → F the sampling loop you actually run.

The whole thing in one frame — three distributions, and the plan

Everything below is about one step backward: you have a noisy \(x_t\) and want a slightly cleaner \(x_{t-1}\). Three distributions describe that step. Learn these names once and the rest is bookkeeping.

① ② ③ — the cast

②\(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\)the true step back — what we want to sampleintractable

The plan in one line: we can't compute ②, so we build ③ out of ① — and it turns out ③ ≈ ②. The rest of this section is why that substitution is not only allowed but exact in the mean.

A. The forward process — pure definition, no network

A1. The noising chain. Pick a tiny variance \(\beta_t\in(0,1)\) per step and define

$$q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})=\mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big),\qquad \alpha_t:=1-\beta_t.$$

Each step shrinks the signal slightly and adds a little Gaussian noise. Run it \(T\) times and \(x_T\) is indistinguishable from pure noise.

A2. The closed‑form jump to any \(t\). Composing Gaussians, you can skip straight from \(x_0\) to \(x_t\):

$$q(x_t\mid x_0)=\mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)I\big),\quad \bar\alpha_t:=\textstyle\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ \ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).$$

This is the only forward fact training uses — it's the marginal, and it's why one network handles all noise levels.

B. The one idea that makes it work — use ① to reach ②

To generate we start at noise \(x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) and undo the chain one step at a time with a learned Gaussian \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) (that's ③). Its ideal target is ②, which is out of reach. Everything hinges on one substitution — and it deserves to be understood in plain words before any formulas, because the rest of the section is just this paragraph made rigorous.

The whole idea, slowly — read this first

The situation. You're standing at a noisy image \(x_t\) and want one small step back toward a cleaner \(x_{t-1}\). If a genie told you the original clean image \(x_0\), there is an exact formula for where \(x_{t-1}\) should sit — its center is \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\). That's distribution ①. The catch: you don't have \(x_0\) — it's what you're generating — and many different clean images could have produced this one \(x_t\).

The honest answer, ②. Not knowing which \(x_0\) it was, the honest distribution for \(x_{t-1}\) must account for every plausible \(x_0\), weighted by how likely it is given \(x_t\). Stacking all those bells is ②, the true reverse step you want to sample. This isn't hand‑waving — it's the literal definition of ②, "average ① over all \(x_0\) that could explain \(x_t\)":

$$②=q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\int \underbrace{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)}_{①}\ \underbrace{q(x_0\mid x_t)}_{\text{weight of each }x_0}\,dx_0.$$

And when you average a pile of bells, the center of the pile = the average of their individual centers. Each ①‑bell is centered at \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\), so:

$$\text{center of ②}=\text{average over plausible }x_0\text{ of }\ \tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0).$$

So ②'s center is completely determined by ① — we just have to do that average. The next line is the shortcut that avoids the integral entirely.

Why one estimate replaces the whole average. Look at the shape of that center — it's a straight line in \(x_0\) (a slope \(b\) times \(x_0\), plus a piece that only uses the \(x_t\) you already hold):

$$\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)=b\,x_0+a(x_t).$$

Straight lines have a magic property: the average of the outputs equals the output at the average input. Concretely, pretend \(\tilde\mu=2x_0+5\) and \(x_0\) could be \(1,3,8\):

$$\underbrace{\tfrac{(2\cdot1+5)+(2\cdot3+5)+(2\cdot8+5)}{3}}_{\text{average the outputs}}=\frac{7+11+21}{3}=13 \ \ =\ \ \underbrace{2\cdot\tfrac{1+3+8}{3}+5}_{\text{plug in the average input}}=2\cdot4+5=13.$$

So you never loop over a million possible \(x_0\)'s. You need one number — the average clean image consistent with \(x_t\), call it \(\hat x_0\) — and plug it in once: \(\ \text{center of ②}=\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0)\). Because the formula is linear, plugging in the estimate doesn't approximate the average — it equals it. Using \(\hat x_0\) is using the expected value.

Where \(\hat x_0\) comes from — the part that connects to training. That number is \(\hat x_0=\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]\), "the average clean image behind \(x_t\)." Two routes to it:

  • Route A (brute force): sample many \(x_0\sim q(x_0\mid x_t)\) and average them — literally the definition of the expected value. Too expensive; we don't do this at inference.
  • Route B (what we do): the network hands it to you, because MSE training is an averaging operation — the value minimizing squared error is provably the conditional mean \(\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]\). Over the dataset, least squares settles the net's output onto the average clean image behind each \(x_t\).

So the optimization is exactly what computes the expected value — same \(\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]\) as brute‑force averaging, done once during training. That is why we plug in the estimate, and what "using it" means: \(\hat x_0\) is the expected clean image, MSE produced it, and — because \(\tilde\mu_t\) is a straight line — dropping it into ①'s formula lands you exactly on ②'s center. Sections C–F below are just this one paragraph made rigorous: C trains the net that produces \(\hat x_0\); D–E show why the Gaussian ③ hugs ② (Gaussian shape, exact mean, width \(\tilde\beta_t\)); F runs the sampler that uses it.

C. Training — from likelihood to a one‑line loss

Goal of training: get the network to output \(\hat x_0=\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]\) (equivalently \(\hat\varepsilon\)), the estimate section B needs. Here is the derivation that turns "maximize likelihood" into "predict the noise by MSE," with the \(x_0\)‑conditioning trick made explicit.

C1. The ELBO splits into per‑step KLs. This is the step people wave through, so here is the whole trick, in five moves.

The ELBO trick, move by move

(i) Can't integrate — so bound it. \(\log p_\theta(x_0)=\log\int p_\theta(x_{0:T})\,dx_{1:T}\) is intractable. Multiply and divide by the forward chain \(q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)\), then push \(\log\) inside the expectation with Jensen:

$$\log p_\theta(x_0)=\log\mathbb E_{q}\!\Big[\tfrac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\Big]\ \ge\ \mathbb E_{q}\Big[\log\tfrac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\Big]\ =:\ \text{ELBO}.$$

(ii) Both joints factor. The reverse model is a Markov chain, and so is the forward one:

$$p_\theta(x_{0:T})=p(x_T)\!\prod_{t\ge1}\! p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t),\qquad q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)=\prod_{t\ge1} q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}).$$

Putting these in the bound gives \(-\log p_\theta(x_0)\le \mathbb E_q\big[-\log p(x_T)+\sum_{t\ge1}\log\tfrac{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})}{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}\big]\).

(iii) The key move — rewrite the forward step conditioned on \(x_0\). Taken as written, \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\) points the "wrong way" and won't pair with \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\). Add \(x_0\) to the conditioning (free, by the Markov property) and flip with Bayes:

$$q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})=q(x_t\mid x_{t-1},x_0)=\underbrace{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)}_{\text{now points backward}}\cdot\frac{q(x_t\mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}.$$

(iv) Telescope. Substitute that for \(t\ge2\). The ratio \(\tfrac{q(x_t\mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}\) is a telescoping product — nearly everything cancels, leaving only the two endpoints \(\tfrac{q(x_T\mid x_0)}{q(x_1\mid x_0)}\). Regrouping the logs into KLs:

$$-\log p_\theta(x_0)\ \le\ \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\|\,p(x_T)\big)}_{L_T\ \text{(no }\theta\text{, }\approx0)} \;+\;\sum_{t>1}\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\big)}_{L_{t-1}} \;\underbrace{-\ \log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)}_{L_0}.$$

(v) What the trick bought us. The intractable target \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) (②) never appears. Every learnable term \(L_{t-1}\) instead compares \(p_\theta\) against the \(x_0\)‑conditioned posterior \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\) — ① — which C2 shows is a closed‑form Gaussian. Conditioning on \(x_0\) is the entire reason the loss is computable.

C2. ① is an exact Gaussian (Bayes). Because conditioning on \(x_0\) makes everything jointly Gaussian, flip it with Bayes (Markov: \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1},x_0)=q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\)):

$$\underbrace{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)}_{①}\ \propto\ \underbrace{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})}_{\mathcal N(\sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1},\beta_t I)}\cdot\underbrace{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}_{\mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})I)}.$$

A product of Gaussians is Gaussian; completing the square gives the closed form in known constants, \(x_0\) and \(x_t\):

$$①=\mathcal N\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0),\,\tilde\beta_t I\big),\quad \tilde\mu_t=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_t,\quad \tilde\beta_t=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t.$$

Flag for §2: that posterior std \(\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}\) is exactly the \(\sigma_t\) DDIM keeps as a free knob — DDPM sits at \(\sigma_t=\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}\), DDIM at \(\sigma_t=0\).

C3. The KL becomes a squared‑mean gap. ① and \(p_\theta\) are Gaussians with the same variance, so their KL is just how far the means sit apart:

$$L_{t-1}=\frac{1}{2\tilde\beta_t}\big\lVert \tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)-\mu_\theta(x_t,t)\big\rVert^2.$$

Training the reverse step therefore means one thing: make \(\mu_\theta\) match the exact posterior mean \(\tilde\mu_t\).

C4. Reparametrize to noise → the final loss. We could train \(\mu_\theta\) to hit \(\tilde\mu_t\) directly, but there's a cleaner target hiding inside. Recall from A2 that \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\), so \(x_0=(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\). Substitute that into \(\tilde\mu_t\) and the \(x_0\) disappears — the posterior mean is just \(x_t\) minus a scaled copy of the noise:

$$\tilde\mu_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon\Big).$$

Now the trick. Give the network's mean the identical shape, with its predicted \(\varepsilon_\theta\) in the one slot where the true \(\varepsilon\) sits:

$$\mu_\theta(x_t,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\Big).$$

Subtract the two. Since \(x_t\) is a known input, it's the same on both sides and cancels — only the noise slot differs:

$$\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta=\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\big(\varepsilon_\theta-\varepsilon\big) \ \Longrightarrow\ \big\lVert\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta\big\rVert^2=\underbrace{\frac{\beta_t^2}{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}}_{\text{a constant in }t}\big\lVert\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta\big\rVert^2.$$

That's what "the mean gap collapses into a noise gap" means: matching the mean is exactly matching the noise, up to a per‑\(t\) scalar. Feed it back into \(L_{t-1}=\tfrac{1}{2\tilde\beta_t}\lVert\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta\rVert^2\), drop that scalar weight (DDPM found unweighted trains better), and you're left with the loss you actually run:

$$\boxed{\;\mathcal L=\mathbb E_{x_0,\,t,\,\varepsilon}\big[\lVert \varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\rVert^2\big]\;}\qquad x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon.$$

Why predict \(\varepsilon\) rather than \(\mu\)? Same information (they're one linear map apart), but \(\varepsilon\) is a fixed unit‑variance target at every noise level — much easier to fit than a \(\mu\) whose scale drifts with \(t\). Note too: nothing here references chaining steps, only the marginal A2 — the hook §2 pulls on.

D. First — is the reverse step even a Gaussian?

Everything from here on models ③ as a Gaussian. That is only legal if the true reverse ② is (nearly) Gaussian — and ② is a mixture of bells, which in general is not. So this question comes before any talk of its mean or variance. The whole logic is short; the detailed proof (two independent derivations) is the deep‑dive box after §E.

Why a small step collapses the mixture to one Gaussian — the whole logic
  1. ② is a pile of bells. One exact Gaussian ① \(=\mathcal N(\tilde\mu_t,\tilde\beta_t)\) per candidate clean image \(x_0\), weighted by \(q(x_0\mid x_t)\). A pile can be skewed or multi‑humped — not obviously Gaussian.
  2. "Gaussian?" ⟺ "are the bells stacked?" Each bell sits at \(\tilde\mu_t=b\,x_0+a(x_t)\), so the pile is one clean bell exactly when the centers are packed tighter than a single bell is wide. Two lengths decide it:
    $$\underbrace{\text{bell width}=\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}=O(\sqrt{\beta_t})}_{\text{how fat each ① is}}\quad\text{vs.}\quad\underbrace{\text{center scatter}=b\times(\text{spread of }x_0)=O(\beta_t)}_{\text{how far the ①'s drift apart}}.$$
  3. A small step wins the race. \(\dfrac{\text{scatter}}{\text{width}}=O(\sqrt{\beta_t})\to0\): the centers pack together faster than the bells narrow, so the bells overlap and the pile becomes one Gaussian. (Equivalently — a narrow center‑cloud convolved with a much wider Gaussian blur is that Gaussian, however ugly the \(x_0\) posterior.)

The objection to kill first: a small step does not pin down \(x_0\) — \(x_t\) still has many possible clean images (\(\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\) stays large). It pins down the immediate predecessor \(x_{t-1}\): since \(x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon\) with \(\beta_t\) tiny, \(x_{t-1}\approx x_t-\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon\) sits in a \(\pm\sqrt{\beta_t}\) sliver whatever \(x_0\) turns out to be. The many \(x_0\) each move that landing point by only \(b\propto\beta_t\to0\) — so the destination stays uncertain while the next step is nearly forced. The \(x_0\) choice is made gradually over \(\sim\!1000\) steps, never in one.

Big steps break it. Jump far and \(b=O(1)\): different \(x_0\) send \(x_{t-1}\) to genuinely different places, ② spreads and can go multimodal. That is why DDPM needs many small steps — and why DDIM's big jumps rely on the exact marginal‑matching argument in §2 instead, not on this one.

This licenses the two numbers §E now pins down: the Gaussian's center (matched to ② exactly, because \(\tilde\mu_t\) is linear in \(x_0\)) and its width (fixed to \(\tilde\beta_t\); the extra scatter term \(b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)=O(\beta_t^2)\) is dropped as higher order). Detailed proof of the collapse: the two‑views deep dive after §E.

E. Where \(x_0\) goes — ②'s center (exact) and its width (\(\tilde\beta_t\))

§D established ② is a Gaussian; now pin its two numbers. The center lands on ②'s center exactly; the width keeps only \(\tilde\beta_t\). Here it is rigorously — Fact 1 (regression → conditional average), Fact 2 (linearity), a check that this average is ②'s center, then ②'s variance and the one honest approximation.

§E in full — the center substitution is exact; the width keeps only \(\tilde\beta_t\)

§B argued it in words; here it is as three checkable facts. \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\) (the mean of ①) carries \(x_0\), yet the thing sampling needs (②) carries none. This box proves the substitution is exact in the mean — Fact 1 (regression → average), Fact 2 (linearity), then a check that the average is ②'s center.

Setup. The network \(\mu_\theta(x_t,t)\) is fed only \(x_t\) — structurally blind to \(x_0\) — and training minimizes the C3 loss averaged over every \((x_0,x_t)\) pair the data produces, \(\min_\theta \mathbb E_{x_0,x_t}\lVert \tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)-\mu_\theta(x_t)\rVert^2\). First read \(\tilde\mu_t\) from C2 as a straight‑line function of \(x_0\) — a fixed slope times \(x_0\), plus a piece that only depends on the known \(x_t\):

$$\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)=\underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}}_{\text{slope }b\ (\text{a number})}\,x_0\ +\ \underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t}_{\text{call it }a(x_t)}\ =\ b\,x_0+a(x_t).$$

Now two facts finish the argument. Take them one at a time.

Fact 1 — a network that only sees \(x_t\) is driven to the conditional mean. Fix an \(x_t\). Its target \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\) is a random vector (random because \(x_0\) is still uncertain given \(x_t\)); the net must commit to one output \(f=\mu_\theta(x_t)\). Which single point is closest, in mean square, to a random vector? Its mean. Add and subtract \(m:=\mathbb E[\tilde\mu_t\mid x_t]\):

$$\mathbb E\big[\lVert\tilde\mu_t-f\rVert^2\mid x_t\big]=\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[\lVert\tilde\mu_t-m\rVert^2\mid x_t\big]}_{\text{fixed, no }f}+\lVert m-f\rVert^2.$$

The cross term vanishes because \(\mathbb E[\tilde\mu_t-m\mid x_t]=0\). Only the last term depends on \(f\), and it's killed by \(f=m\). So the optimum is \(\mu_\theta^\star(x_t)=\mathbb E[\tilde\mu_t\mid x_t]\) — this is the whole reason MSE regression returns a conditional average.

Fact 2 — for a straight‑line function, "average then evaluate" = "evaluate at the average". Push the expectation through the affine form (constants \(a,b\) come straight out):

$$\mathbb E[\tilde\mu_t\mid x_t]=\mathbb E[b\,x_0+a(x_t)\mid x_t]=b\,\underbrace{\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]}_{=\ \hat x_0}+a(x_t)=\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0).$$

That is where \(x_0\) goes: it sits inside an expectation, and because \(\tilde\mu_t\) is linear the expectation lands on \(\hat x_0\). The net outputs \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0)\) — the true \(x_0\) is never needed, only its conditional mean, which the net supplies. (If \(\tilde\mu_t\) were curved in \(x_0\) this would only be approximate — a Jensen gap — but here it's exactly linear.)

The check we actually care about. Facts 1–2 fixed our model's center: \(\mu_\theta=\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0)\). But the distribution we truly wish we could sample is ②. So the real question is: is our center in the right place — does \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0)\) equal the center of ②? To answer, we need ②'s center, so let's compute it. (Yes, you want the mean of \(p_\theta\) at runtime — we already have it, \(\mu_\theta\); this step is the verification that \(\mu_\theta\) lands on the true target.)

Computing ②'s center means knowing how ② is built. Sampling \(x_{t-1}\) from ② is a two‑step recipe:

$$\text{(1) draw a clean image }x_0\sim q(x_0\mid x_t);\qquad\text{(2) draw }x_{t-1}\sim ①(x_0)=\mathcal N\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0),\,\tilde\beta_t I\big).$$

Each \(x_0\) gives a bell centered at \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\); ② is the pile‑up of all those bells — a mixture.

Center of ② — average it in two stages (law of total expectation). The center of ② is \(\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t]\) (since ② is the distribution \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\)). Averaging over the recipe means: first average inside the bell you landed in (that returns its center \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\)), then average those centers over which \(x_0\) the first step drew:

$$\underbrace{\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t]}_{\text{center of ②}}=\mathbb E_{x_0\mid x_t}\big[\ \underbrace{\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0]}_{\text{inner: bell's center }=\ \tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)}\ \big]\overset{\text{Fact 2}}{=}\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0).$$

The two \(\mathbb E\)'s differ only in what they condition on: the outer \(\mathbb E[\,\cdot\mid x_t]\) is what we want (average given just \(x_t\)); the inner \(\mathbb E[\,\cdot\mid x_t,x_0]\) also fixes \(x_0\), giving one bell's center. The law of total expectation says the outer one = average of the inner ones over \(x_0\) — which is why the two‑stage split is exact.

No typo — \(q\) vs \(\mathbb E\) are two different things from the same conditioning. \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)=①\) is the whole distribution (the entire bell). \(\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0]\) is a single number: the mean of that bell — i.e. "average \(x_{t-1}\) over the distribution ①," which by definition is its center \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\). So the inner \(\mathbb E\) is computed using ①; it just extracts where ① is centered. One is the bell, the other is the bell's center.

That's the punchline: ②'s center = \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0)\) = our model's center \(\mu_\theta\). So the \(\mu\) we plug into the sampler doesn't just center some Gaussian — it lands exactly on the center of the true reverse step we wanted. \(p_\theta\) is aimed correctly.

Spread of ② — law of total variance. Its spread is \(\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) (again because ② is \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\)). The same two layers, for variance, add instead of collapse:

$$\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}_{\text{spread of ②}}=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{x_0\mid x_t}\!\big[\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\big]}_{=\ \tilde\beta_t\ \text{(each bell's own width)}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_{x_0\mid x_t}\!\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\big)}_{=\ b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\ \text{(centers scatter)}}.$$
Where those two terms come from — the derivation

\(x_t\) is fixed throughout this whole derivation (we're computing one step, at one \(x_t\)), so to keep the algebra readable we write the bell center \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\) as just \(\mu(x_0)\) — the \(x_t\) is still there, only suppressed. With that, two shorthands (per coordinate): the bell you landed in has center \(\mu(x_0):=\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0]=\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\) and width \(\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)=\tilde\beta_t\); the whole pile has center \(m:=\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t]\) — this expectation is taken under ② itself, i.e. under \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\), since \(m\) is just ②'s mean (the true reverse, not the model \(p_\theta\)). Variance is average squared distance from \(m\):

$$\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\mathbb E\big[(x_{t-1}-m)^2\mid x_t\big].$$

The one trick: split the gap through the bell's own center \(\mu(x_0)\) — add and subtract it:

$$x_{t-1}-m=\underbrace{(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))}_{\text{how far from your bell's center}}+\underbrace{(\mu(x_0)-m)}_{\text{how far that bell's center is from the pile's}}.$$

Square it — three pieces — and average, doing the inner \(x_0\)‑fixed average first (tower rule):

$$\mathbb E\big[(x_{t-1}-m)^2\big]=\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))^2\big]}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{2\,\mathbb E\big[(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))(\mu(x_0)-m)\big]}_{\text{(II) cross term}}+\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(\mu(x_0)-m)^2\big]}_{\text{(III)}}.$$
  • (I) → the within term. Fix \(x_0\): \(\mathbb E[(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))^2\mid x_t,x_0]\) is exactly one bell's variance \(\tilde\beta_t\). Averaging a constant over \(x_0\) leaves \(\tilde\beta_t\).
  • (II) → 0. This is the important one, and it is not \(\mathbb E[AB]=\mathbb E[A]\,\mathbb E[B]\) (that needs independence, which we don't have). It's the tower rule. See below the list for the full statement and application.
  • (III) → the between term. \(\mu(x_0)\) is a function of the random \(x_0\); its average over \(x_0\) is exactly \(m\) (shown just below), so \(\mathbb E[(\mu(x_0)-m)^2]\) is — by the very definition of variance — \(\operatorname{Var}_{x_0\mid x_t}(\mu(x_0))\), the scatter of the bell centers.

Why \(\mathbb E[\mu(x_0)]=m\) (needed for (III)). "Average of the bell centers = the overall center" is the tower rule once more — \(\mu(x_0)\) is itself an inner expectation, so averaging it over \(x_0\) rebuilds the plain mean:

$$\mathbb E_{x_0\mid x_t}\big[\mu(x_0)\big]=\mathbb E_{x_0\mid x_t}\big[\ \mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0]\ \big]\overset{\text{tower}}{=}\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t]=m.$$

That's what lets us write (III) as a genuine variance: \(\operatorname{Var}(\mu(x_0))=\mathbb E[(\mu(x_0)-\mathbb E[\mu(x_0)])^2]=\mathbb E[(\mu(x_0)-m)^2]\), since \(\mathbb E[\mu(x_0)]=m\). (Same identity we used for ②'s mean earlier — the centers average back to \(m\).)

Term (II) in full — the tower rule, stated then used. First the rule itself (law of iterated expectations): for any two random variables and any function \(g\), you may average in two stages — inner average with \(Y\) fixed, then outer average over \(Y\):

$$\boxed{\ \mathbb E[\,g(X,Y)\,]=\mathbb E_{Y}\big[\ \mathbb E[\,g(X,Y)\mid Y\,]\ \big]\ }$$

Here \(X=x_{t-1}\), \(Y=x_0\) (everything conditioned on \(x_t\)), and \(g=(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))(\mu(x_0)-m)\). Apply the rule — condition on \(x_0\) inside:

$$\mathbb E\big[(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))(\mu(x_0)-m)\big]=\mathbb E_{x_0}\Big[\ \mathbb E\big[(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))(\mu(x_0)-m)\ \big|\ x_t,x_0\big]\ \Big].$$

Now work on the inner expectation, where \(x_0\) is held fixed. There \((\mu(x_0)-m)\) is a constant, so it pulls out (constant factor leaving an expectation — plain linearity, not independence):

$$\mathbb E\big[(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))(\mu(x_0)-m)\mid x_t,x_0\big]=(\mu(x_0)-m)\cdot\mathbb E\big[x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0)\mid x_t,x_0\big].$$

And that last expectation is \(0\), because \(\mu(x_0)=\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0]\) is by definition the mean of \(x_{t-1}\) in that bell: \(\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0]-\mu(x_0)=0\). Feeding \(0\) back to the outer average kills the whole term:

$$\mathbb E\big[(x_{t-1}-\mu(x_0))(\mu(x_0)-m)\big]=\mathbb E_{x_0}\big[(\mu(x_0)-m)\cdot 0\big]=0.$$

So (II) vanishes by conditioning, not by factoring a product — which is exactly why (I) and (III) add with no overlap.

Put the survivors back together:

$$\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\underbrace{\tilde\beta_t}_{\text{(I) within}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_{x_0\mid x_t}\!\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\big)}_{\text{(III) between}}.$$

Finally the between term simplifies: \(\tilde\mu_t=b\,x_0+a(x_t)\) is affine, and adding the constant \(a(x_t)\) doesn't change variance, so \(\operatorname{Var}(b\,x_0+a)=b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\). That's the \(b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\) in the boxed line above.

What are \(b^2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\)?

  • \(b\) is the slope of the bell center in \(x_0\): from C2, \(b=\dfrac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\). It's "how far the center \(\tilde\mu_t\) moves when \(x_0\) moves." Scaling a random variable by \(b\) scales its variance by \(b^2\) — that's where the square comes from.
  • \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\) is the spread of the clean image given the noisy one — the posterior \(q(x_0\mid x_t)\). It measures how uncertain you still are about the true \(x_0\) after seeing \(x_t\): tiny at low noise (\(x_t\) nearly pins \(x_0\)), large at high noise (many clean images could explain \(x_t\)).

So the between term = (how much \(x_0\) moves the center)\(^2\) × (how uncertain \(x_0\) is) — both factors shrink toward fine, low‑noise steps.

So what happened to the second term in \(p_\theta\)?

Nothing cancelled it — we drop it on purpose. \(p_\theta\)'s variance is a modeling choice, and DDPM sets it to the first term only:

$$\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\underbrace{\tilde\beta_t}_{p_\theta\ \text{keeps this}}+\underbrace{b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)}_{p_\theta\ \text{ignores this}}.$$

So \(p_\theta\) is deliberately a little too narrow — it under‑covers ②'s true spread by exactly \(b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\). This is the one place ③ ≠ ② (the mean matched exactly; the variance is slightly short).

Why give it up — is it just hard to compute? Two reasons, and the first is basically "yes, hard":

  • It's intractable, and the net doesn't even give it to you. \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\) is the spread of all clean images consistent with \(x_t\) — a property of the true data distribution, no closed form (the same wall that makes ② intractable). And an MSE‑trained network outputs only the mean \(\hat x_0\), not its uncertainty. A plain regressor gives a point, not a spread — so the very quantity you'd need isn't available from the model as trained.
  • It's small, so it isn't worth the trouble. At fine steps \(x_t\) nearly pins down \(x_0\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\approx 0\). Fixing the variance to \(\tilde\beta_t\) is simpler and more stable than predicting one, and the error is tiny.

It's not impossible, though: Improved‑DDPM adds a second output head that predicts the variance (an interpolation between the bounds \(\tilde\beta_t\) and \(\beta_t\)), recovering most of that term and improving likelihood. So — exact is intractable and absent from the net; fixed‑to‑\(\tilde\beta_t\) is simple and good enough; learned is a modest upgrade later.

Why two terms that add (unlike the mean)? Because the pile‑up gets its spread from two independent sources, and spreads from independent sources stack:

  • Within each bell: even if all bells were centered at the same spot, each one still has its own width \(\tilde\beta_t\). That alone gives the pile a spread of \(\tilde\beta_t\).
  • Between the bells: the bells are not centered at the same spot — different \(x_0\) puts the center at a different place \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\). That scatter of centers spreads the pile out further, by \(b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\).

Total spread = within + between. Contrast with the mean: the balance point of the pile only cares where the centers sit on average — a single average, one term. Spread cares about both how fat each bell is and how far apart the bells are — two terms.

Concrete: mix two bells, \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(\mathcal N(10,1)\), 50/50. Each bell's width is just \(1\), but the mixture obviously sprawls from \(\sim0\) to \(\sim10\). Total variance \(=\underbrace{1}_{\text{within}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\{0,10\}}_{=\,25\ \text{between}}=26\) — far bigger than either bell. The mean, meanwhile, is just \(\tfrac{0+10}{2}=5\). That gap is exactly the "centers scatter" term.

For us the "between" term \(b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\) is small — chiefly because at fine steps the coefficient \(b\to0\) (a tiny step barely moves the landing point as \(x_0\) varies), and additionally because at low noise \(x_0\) is itself well‑determined. Either way the centers barely scatter and the pile is essentially one bell of width \(\tilde\beta_t\). That's why \(p_\theta\) (which uses only \(\tilde\beta_t\)) is a good match, and why the mean — which never depended on that term — is matched exactly. Net effect: \(p_\theta\) matches ②'s mean exactly and its variance almost exactly.

You wanted ②; you got a Gaussian centered exactly on ②, built entirely from a network that never sees \(x_0\).

So what do we actually "have" now? A concrete, computable distribution for the reverse step:

$$p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\mathcal N\big(\underbrace{\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0)}_{\mu:\ \text{net gives this}},\ \underbrace{\tilde\beta_t}_{\sigma^2:\ \text{schedule gives this}}\,I\big)\ \approx\ q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=②.$$

That's the whole answer to "how do we get \(p(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\)": you don't store the intractable ② — you get a Gaussian whose center comes from the network (\(\mu\)) and whose width comes from the schedule (\(\sigma\)), and it hugs ②. To generate, you now just draw one sample from this Gaussian — and that single mechanical act is the "\(\mu+\sigma z\)" you keep seeing. Section F makes that explicit.

Deep dive — the two independent proofs that the mixture collapses (the detail behind §D)

§D stated the collapse and gave the one‑paragraph reason (scatter \(\ll\) width). Here are the two full derivations it pointed to: View 1 reaches the Gaussian from Bayes (a razor‑thin likelihood times the prior); View 2 reaches it from the mixture (the coefficient \(b\)). They are the same fact, proved from opposite ends.

View 1 — Bayes with a razor‑thin likelihood. We'll build this in five steps, in order: set up the question, get the two ingredients, combine them, watch it become a bell, then confirm with algebra and say when it's exact.

Step 1 — the question and the tool. We're at a fixed noisy image \(x_t\) and want the distribution over the cleaner image it came from, \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\). Bayes rewrites it as two things we can reason about:

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\ \propto\ \underbrace{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})}_{\text{likelihood}}\ \cdot\ \underbrace{q(x_{t-1})}_{\text{prior}}.$$

The whole confusion is "what's fixed." \(x_t\) is fixed — it's the one noisy image in your hand, a constant. \(x_{t-1}\) is the unknown: we test every candidate value \(y\) for it. So both factors above are read as functions of the candidate \(y\), with \(x_t\) frozen. The answer \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) is a curve over candidates — one height per \(y\).

Step 2 — ingredient one: the likelihood is a single narrow bump. \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\) is a Gaussian — but Gaussian in which variable? With \(x_t\) frozen and \(y=x_{t-1}\) the variable:

$$L(y):=q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}{=}y)\ \propto\ \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(y-m)^2}{2s^2}\Big),\qquad m=\frac{x_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}},\quad s=\sqrt{\beta_t}.$$

It's one bump over candidates, centered at \(m=x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\), width \(s=\sqrt{\beta_t}\). It answers: "for the \(x_t\) I'm holding, which candidate \(y\) most plausibly noised into it?" A small step (\(\beta_t\) tiny) makes it razor‑thin — near zero except in a \(\pm\sqrt{\beta_t}\) sliver around \(m\). (Careful: if instead you froze \(x_{t-1}\) and varied \(x_t\) you'd get a different Gaussian for each \(x_{t-1}\) — the forward reading. The reverse freezes \(x_t\), so it's a single bump. Same formula, opposite slot held fixed.)

Step 3 — ingredient two: the prior, and how they combine (pointwise). \(P(y):=q(x_{t-1}{=}y)\) is the messy data density over candidates. The posterior is the two curves multiplied height‑by‑height: at each candidate \(y\), \(f(y)=L(y)\,P(y)\). No multiplication across candidates — each \(y\) is scored on its own, and the scores are the curve. Concretely, hold \(x_t{=}5\) so \(L\) bumps at 5; take a gently rising prior:

candidate \(y=x_{t-1}\)4.04.55.05.56.0
\(L(y)\) — explains the 5?0.00030.1351.000.1350.0003
\(P(y)\) — plausible?1.201.251.301.351.40
score \(f=L\cdot P\)~00.171.300.18~0

The score row \(\sim0,\,0.17,\,1.30,\,0.18,\,\sim0\) is a bell near 5 — that curve is \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t{=}5)\). Notice: \(x_t{=}5\) is the same in every column; only the candidate \(y\) changes; and the multiply is once per column. (Hold \(x_t{=}8\) instead → the same sweep gives a bell near 8. One bell per \(x_t\) — that's what "the reverse is Gaussian" means.)

Step 4 — why the product is a bell: the spotlight. \(L\) is a razor‑thin flashlight beam aimed at \(m\); \(f=L\cdot P\) is "the prior, but only where the beam shines." Through a tiny beam you see only a tiny patch of the prior — and a tiny patch of any smooth curve is just a straight ramp (constant + slight tilt). Bell \(\times\) constant \(=\) same bell; bell \(\times\) gentle tilt \(=\) same bell, shifted a hair. Either way, one bump. The prior can't add a second bump, because everywhere it might want one (far from \(m\)) the beam has already set \(f\approx0\). That's why even a bimodal prior (mass at 0 and 10) gives a clean Gaussian: a thin beam at \(m{=}3\) never reaches the modes — they're invisible. (Only a wide beam — a big step — straddles both and comes out non‑Gaussian. Hence small steps stay Gaussian, big jumps don't.)

Step 5 — the algebra confirms it, line by line. Over the sliver, Taylor‑expand the log‑prior to first order: \(\log P(y)\approx \text{const}+g\,(y-m)\), with \(g=\tfrac{d}{dy}\log P|_m\) the score. Now multiply the two factors and simplify — nothing skipped.

(a) Multiply, using \(e^A e^B=e^{A+B}\). The prior's constant just rescales, so fold it into "\(\text{const}\)":

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\ \propto\ \underbrace{e^{-\frac{(y-m)^2}{2s^2}}}_{L(y)}\cdot\underbrace{e^{\,g(y-m)}}_{P(y)\text{ over sliver}}=\exp\!\Big(\underbrace{-\frac{(y-m)^2}{2s^2}+g(y-m)}_{\text{call this }E}\Big).$$

(b) Substitute \(u=y-m\) to declutter, and factor \(-\tfrac{1}{2s^2}\) out of the exponent \(E\):

$$E=-\frac{u^2}{2s^2}+g\,u=-\frac{1}{2s^2}\big(u^2-2s^2 g\,u\big).$$

Check the factor: \(-\tfrac{1}{2s^2}\cdot u^2=-\tfrac{u^2}{2s^2}\) ✓, and \(-\tfrac{1}{2s^2}\cdot(-2s^2 g\,u)=g\,u\) ✓.

(c) Complete the square inside the parentheses using \(u^2-2au=(u-a)^2-a^2\) with \(a=s^2g\):

$$u^2-2s^2 g\,u=(u-s^2 g)^2-(s^2 g)^2.$$

(d) Put it back. The \(-(s^2g)^2\) piece has no \(u\) — it's a constant, so it joins "\(\text{const}\)":

$$E=-\frac{1}{2s^2}\Big[(u-s^2 g)^2-(s^2g)^2\Big]=-\frac{(u-s^2 g)^2}{2s^2}+\underbrace{\frac{(s^2g)^2}{2s^2}}_{\text{const}}.$$

(e) Undo \(u=y-m\) and exponentiate — the constant becomes an overall scale (absorbed by normalization):

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\ \propto\ \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\big(y-(m+s^2 g)\big)^2}{2s^2}\Big)=\mathcal N\big(\underbrace{m}_{\text{denoise}}+\underbrace{s^2 g}_{\text{score nudge}},\ \ s^2\big).$$

It's a perfect square in \(y\) → still Gaussian, just re‑centered from \(m\) to \(m+s^2g\), same width \(s=\sqrt{\beta_t}\). Read the answer: center \(m=x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\) undoes the scaling (denoise); \(+s^2g=+\beta_t\cdot\text{score}\) leans toward denser regions. This is literally "reverse mean = denoise \(+\) a score correction," and it fell straight out of "narrow bump \(\times\) exp(linear)."

When is it exact? — the leftover. The one thing we dropped is the prior's curvature (the quadratic Taylor term). On the natural window \(y-m\sim\sqrt{\beta_t}\) the exponent's terms rank by size:

$$\underbrace{-\frac{(y-m)^2}{2\beta_t}}_{O(1)\,:\ \text{sets the bell}}\ \gg\ \underbrace{g(y-m)}_{O(\sqrt{\beta_t})\,:\ \text{score, keeps mean right}}\ \gg\ \underbrace{\tfrac12 g'(y-m)^2}_{O(\beta_t)\,:\ \text{curvature, would bend it}}.$$

Both the score and the curvature vanish as \(\beta_t\to0\), but the curvature is one order smaller — so we keep the score and drop the curvature not because it's "small" but because it's higher order. (And we keep the small score because, summed over the \(\sim1/\beta_t\) steps, its per‑step nudge \(\beta_t g\) accumulates to \(O(1)\) — it's what carries you from noise to data; the curvature error summed the same way still \(\to0\).) Hence: the reverse step is Gaussian to leading order, and exactly Gaussian as \(\beta_t\to0\).

View 2 — the mixture, via the coefficient \(b\). ② is the pile of bells centered at \(\tilde\mu_t=b\,x_0+a(x_t)\); it's non‑Gaussian only because those centers scatter as \(x_0\) varies, by \(b\times(\text{spread of }x_0)\). What kills the scatter in a small step is \(b\to0\):

$$b=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\ \xrightarrow[\ \beta_t\to0\ ]{}\ 0,\qquad\text{so between‑scatter}=b^2\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\to0.$$

But \(b\to0\) alone isn't the argument — compared to what? A pile of bells is one bell only if the centers are packed tighter than a single bell is wide. Line up the two lengths:

$$\underbrace{\text{width of one bell}=\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}=O(\sqrt{\beta_t})}_{\text{how fat each ① is}}\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad\underbrace{\text{scatter of centers}=b\times(\text{spread of }x_0)=O(\beta_t)}_{\text{how far the ①'s drift apart}}.$$

Their ratio \(\dfrac{\text{scatter}}{\text{width}}=O(\sqrt{\beta_t})\to0\): the centers pack together faster than the bells narrow, so the bells overlap ever more and the pile collapses onto one bell. Equivalently, ② is a narrow center‑cloud (width \(\sim\beta_t\)) convolved with a much wider Gaussian blur (width \(\sim\sqrt{\beta_t}\)) — and smearing any shape by a Gaussian far wider than itself yields a Gaussian, however ugly the \(x_0\) posterior was. That is the "why Gaussian," not merely \(b\to0\).

Correction to keep honest: it is not that \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0\mid x_t)\to0\) — the uncertainty about the clean image depends on the absolute noise level, and at high noise it stays large no matter how small the step. What vanishes is \(b\): a tiny step barely moves the landing point as \(x_0\) changes, so all the bells pile at nearly the same place even though \(x_0\) is still very uncertain.

Both views are the same sentence: one infinitesimal step can't reveal much about which clean image you're heading toward, so the reverse step can't split into modes — it stays a single Gaussian.

Why big steps break it. Jump far (\(\bar\alpha_t\to\bar\alpha_s\), \(s\ll t\)) and \(b\) is \(O(1)\): different \(x_0\)'s send \(x_{t-1}\) to genuinely different places, so ② spreads and can go multimodal — not Gaussian. The extreme case: "reverse" straight from pure noise to data is the whole multimodal data distribution. That's why DDPM needs many small steps — and why DDIM's big jumps cannot use this Gaussian argument; they rely on the exact marginal‑matching argument in §2 instead.

F. Sampling — the ancestral loop, step by step (this is inference)

Now \(x_0\) is unknown — it's what we're generating. Here is exactly where the estimate enters: we compute the posterior mean \(\tilde\mu_t\), but with the network's reconstructed \(\hat x_0\) in place of the \(x_0\) we don't have.

The sampler, in full
  1. Start: \(x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\).
  2. For \(t=T,\dots,1\):
    • predict noise \(\hat\varepsilon=\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\);
    • reconstruct clean image \(\hat x_0=\dfrac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\), then clamp to \([-1,1]\) (the scar below);
    • form the posterior mean with the estimate: \(\mu=\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0)\);
    • draw \(z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) if \(t>1\), else \(z=0\), and step: \(x_{t-1}=\mu+\sigma_t\,z\), with \(\sigma_t=\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}\).
  3. Return \(x_0\).
Why the sampler does each thing — especially the added noise

Why we estimate \(\mu\) at all. The reverse step we want is \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma_t^2 I)\). Its center \(\mu\) is the exact posterior mean \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\) from C2 — but that formula needs \(x_0\), which we're still generating. So we substitute the net's reconstruction: run \(\varepsilon_\theta\), back out \(\hat x_0\), and plug it in. \(\mu\) is now our best guess for "where the slightly‑cleaner image sits."

Why we don't just output \(\mu\). \(\mu\) is only the center of a distribution, not the answer. Many different \(x_{t-1}\) could have produced this \(x_t\); \(\mu\) is their average. If you always took the average you'd blend all those possibilities together every step and drift into a blurry, washed‑out image (the mean of the data, not a sample from it).

Why the added \(\sigma_t z\) — and why it is in the equations. The \(z\) is not invented on the side; it's the second half of the distribution the equations already gave us. Recall the reverse step is a full Gaussian, with both a center and a width — and both came out of C2's closed‑form posterior:

$$p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\mathcal N\big(\underbrace{\tilde\mu_t(x_t,\hat x_0)}_{\mu\ \text{— from C4}},\ \underbrace{\tilde\beta_t}_{\sigma_t^2\ \text{— from C2}}\,I\big).$$

Where does that variance come from? Straight from §E — no repeat of the derivation here: we fix \(\sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t\) (①'s closed‑form width), which is ②'s variance minus the higher‑order scatter term §E dropped. Not learned, not tuned. (Improved‑DDPM later learns a blend of DDPM's two brackets \(\tilde\beta_t\) and \(\beta_t\); vanilla just fixes \(\tilde\beta_t\).)

Now, to generate we need a sample from this Gaussian, not its center \(\mu\). And drawing from any Gaussian has one universal form — start from a plain unit bell \(z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), scale by the width, shift by the center:

$$x\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma_t^2 I)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x=\mu+\sigma_t\,z,\quad z\sim\mathcal N(0,I).$$

So \(\mu+\sigma_t z\) is not an extra step bolted on — it is literally "evaluate the two numbers the equations give (\(\mu=\tilde\mu_t\), \(\sigma_t=\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}\)), then take one draw from that Gaussian." Drop the \(z\) and you'd be outputting the mean of \(p_\theta\), not a sample from it. The loop repeats this draw down to \(t=1\).

So the two pieces have clean jobs: \(\mu\) says where the next image most likely sits; \(\sigma_t z\) is the honest jitter for "I don't know exactly which \(x_{t-1}\) produced this \(x_t\) — here's a random plausible one." Injected fresh each step, that jitter keeps the trajectory exploring and lets the chain reproduce the full variety of the data instead of collapsing to one blurry average. This is why DDPM is a stochastic sampler — and why changing the seed changes the image.

Why \(z=0\) on the last step (\(t=1\)). The final output is your clean image; you want its best estimate, not a re‑noised version of it. Adding noise at \(t=1\) would just dirty the answer you're about to return, so the last step takes the mean only.

DDIM's move (§2) is now one sentence: keep \(\mu\) exactly as above, but set \(\sigma_t=0\) on every step — drop the jitter entirely. Same \(\mu\), zero noise, deterministic result.

One‑line bridge to §2

DDPM vs DDIM, without jargon: both feed the same \(\hat x_0\) into the same posterior ①. They differ only in how much fresh noise \(\sigma_t\) they add back on the step — DDPM adds \(\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}\) (stochastic), DDIM adds \(0\) (deterministic). Same estimate, same target, one different knob. §2 derives why that knob is free to turn.

The scar worth knowing

At the top timesteps \(1/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\) is enormous, so a tiny \(\varepsilon\) error throws \(x_0\) far outside \([-1,1]\) and the chain diverges into noise. The fix — clamp the predicted \(x_0\) to \([-1,1]\) every step (clip_denoised) — moved sample MSE from 0.61 → 0.043 in this repo. It's in both the DDPM and DDIM samplers.

2DDIM — why is it valid?

DDPM samples by crawling \(\sim\!1000\) stochastic steps. DDIM keeps the same trained network but samples deterministically (same seed → same image) in 20–50 steps — no retraining. Two changes, one question: why is that allowed? The answer is a single lever, then a short derivation.

DDIM in plain words (read before the derivation)

The idea: move all the randomness to the very first step.

  • DDPM rolls dice at every step — fresh \(\sigma z\) each time. The final image is random because of all those little injections along the way.
  • DDIM rolls dice once, at the start (\(x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\)), then follows a fixed deterministic recipe down to the image. Same \(x_T\) → same image. It's a change of variables: Gaussian noise in one end, a fixed transform, a data sample out the other.

The catch — you can't just delete DDPM's noise. Setting \(\sigma{=}0\) in DDPM's own step (output the mean every step) does not sample the right distribution — it biases toward blur. DDIM removes the noise and retunes the step coefficient so the distribution stays exactly right (that's the \(c=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\) in Step 2 — with \(\sigma_t{=}0\) it forces a specific \(c\) that compensates for the missing noise). Delete noise + retune coefficient = DDIM; delete noise alone = broken.

Why it still lands on the real distribution: the deterministic recipe is built so that at every noise level the spread of \(x_t\) it produces (as \(x_T\) varies) exactly matches the correct marginal there. Per‑trajectory it's deterministic; over all \(x_T\) the outputs are distributed like the data. That marginal‑matching is the whole guarantee — and it's what the derivation below nails down.

Roadmap — what we'll show, how, and where it clicks

What we'll show: the DDIM update is a legal sampler that reuses the DDPM weights unchanged — deterministic, and still correct when you skip steps.

How, in four moves:

  1. The lever — the DDPM loss only pinned the marginals \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), never how steps chain.
  2. Build the family — write every reverse step with a free noise knob \(\sigma_t\); force it to keep those marginals. One equation fixes everything except \(\sigma_t\).
  3. Pick the member — \(\sigma_t={}\)DDPM std ⟹ DDPM; \(\sigma_t{=}0\) ⟹ DDIM.
  4. Write the update — set \(\sigma_t{=}0\), plug in the net's \(\hat x_0\); notice adjacency was never used ⟹ you may skip steps.

Where it clicks: Step 2 — the instant the marginal‑matching equation leaves \(\sigma_t\) free. That one free parameter is the entire DDPM↔DDIM family: same weights, same marginals, one dial. The table below shows the single line that dial changes.

The lever — the loss only fixed the marginals, not the chaining

The one thing we reuse from §1 is the DDPM loss and its sampling relation:

$$\mathcal L=\mathbb E_{x_0,\,t,\,\varepsilon}\big[\lVert \varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\rVert^2\big],\qquad x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon.$$

It touches only the marginal \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\): corrupt a clean image to one noise level, regress the noise. It never sees \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) — it never constrains how consecutive levels are chained. So the network learned only "at level \(t\), what noise is present," and is blind to the chaining.

Consequence. DDPM's Markov chain is just one chaining consistent with those marginals. Any other chaining with the same marginals is served by the exact same weights — for free, no retraining. DDIM is simply a different chaining with those marginals: one that happens to be deterministic and skippable. Everything below just constructs it.

Step 1 — set up a general reverse step and count the knobs

The strategy first. We don't know the formula for a good reverse step yet, so we won't guess one. Instead we write down a general reverse step with unknown knobs, then (in Step 2) impose the one requirement that makes it valid — it must reproduce the forward marginals \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\) the network was trained on. Whatever knobs survive that requirement are our menu of legal samplers.

Analogy: to find "all right triangles with hypotenuse 5," you don't test triangles one by one — you write \(a^2+b^2=25\) (one equation, two unknowns) and a one‑parameter family survives: pick \(a\), and \(b\) is forced. We're about to do the same: write a general step (two unknowns), impose one constraint, and a one‑parameter family of samplers survives.

The mindset: we're inventing a process. We get to define the reverse step \(q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\) however we like. It must obey exactly one law: the marginals it induces must equal \(q(x_t\mid x_0)=\mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_t)I)\) — the thing the network learned. Every choice below is either the most general form that can satisfy that law, or is already forced by it.

(i) We choose the step to be a Gaussian with a linear mean. This is the one design decision, and it has four small beats:

  • We're free to choose the step's shape. Only the marginals are forced on us; the shape of the reverse step is ours to pick.
  • A Gaussian is just two numbers. A general distribution is a whole curve (a height at every point — infinitely many numbers). A Gaussian is pinned by only its center (mean) and its width (variance). Two numbers.
  • So "match a whole distribution" shrinks to "match two numbers." Our step must output the correct distribution for \(x_{t-1}\). If it's Gaussian, two Gaussians are identical exactly when their center and width agree — so the whole task becomes: match the mean (one equation) and match the variance (one equation). That is why Steps 1–2 are just a few lines.
  • Why the mean must be linear. For the output to actually come out Gaussian, the mean must be a straight‑line (linear) function of the inputs — linear maps keep Gaussians Gaussian. A curvy mean could bend the output into a non‑Gaussian shape and wreck the "two numbers" trick.

So we take \(\text{mean}=A\,x_0+B\,x_t\) — the most general straight‑line function of the two things the step conditions on — with \(A,B\) two free numbers.

All of this is "given \(x_0\)": we work at a fixed clean image throughout, so the data density \(q(x_0)\) (which is not Gaussian) never enters. And DDPM's true posterior (§1‑C2) already had exactly this linear‑Gaussian form — we're just keeping its coefficients free instead of fixed.

(ii) Relabel the mean using the noise coordinate \(\varepsilon\) — pure bookkeeping. It's tidier to measure the \(x_t\)‑part along the "noise direction." Define \(\varepsilon:=\dfrac{x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\), i.e. \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\). Knowing \((x_0,x_t)\) is the same as knowing \((x_0,\varepsilon)\) — reversible. Substitute \(x_t\) into \(A x_0+B x_t\) and collect:

$$A\,x_0+B\,x_t=A\,x_0+B\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\big)=\underbrace{(A+B\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t})}_{\text{call it }A'}\,x_0+\underbrace{B\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}_{\text{call it }c}\,\varepsilon.$$

So the general linear mean is just \(A'x_0+c\,\varepsilon\) — still two free numbers (\(A',c\) renamed from \(A,B\)). Nothing changed but the labels.

(iii) Impose the law. Three clean parts: (A) what our step is, (B) which distribution the law constrains, (C) match and read off \(A'\).

A. Our step is a Gaussian = a center plus a spread. From (i)–(ii):

$$q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)=\mathcal N\big(\underbrace{A'x_0+c\,\varepsilon}_{\text{center (mean)}},\ \underbrace{\sigma_t^2 I}_{\text{spread (variance)}}\big).$$
  • Why \(A'x_0+c\varepsilon\) is the "center": it's the mean of the Gaussian we chose — the single most likely spot for \(x_{t-1}\).
  • Why we "add spread" \(\sigma_t z\): a Gaussian is a cloud, not a point. To draw one sample you take center \(+\) spread\(\,\times\,\)unit noise — the §1 fact "sample \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) \(=\mu+\sigma z\)." So one draw of the step is \(x_{t-1}=(A'x_0+c\varepsilon)+\sigma_t z\). The \(\sigma_t z\) isn't extra — it's how you realize the spread; the center alone would be just the peak, not a sample.

B. Go from the conditional \(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0\) to the marginal \(x_{t-1}\mid x_0\) — marginalize over \(x_t\). Our step is written given \(x_t\), but the law we must satisfy is on the marginal given only \(x_0\). To drop \(x_t\) from the conditioning, average the conditional over all possible \(x_t\), weighted by how likely each is — that's what "marginalize" means. In one line:

$$\boxed{\;q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)=\mathbb E_{\,x_t\sim q(x_t\mid x_0)}\big[\,q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,\big]=\int q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,q(x_t\mid x_0)\,dx_t.\;}$$

The marginal is the average of the conditional over \(x_t\). We don't grind the integral — everything is Gaussian, so we use a shortcut: \(\varepsilon\) is how we sample \(x_t\). Since \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\), drawing \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) is drawing \(x_t\sim q(x_t\mid x_0)\). So averaging over \(x_t\) = averaging over \(\varepsilon\):

$$\varepsilon\ \text{fixed}\ \big(\text{one specific }x_t\big)\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{let it range}\ }\ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\ \big(\text{all }x_t,\text{ weighted}\big)\ =\ \text{marginalize over }x_t.$$

So the only change from conditional to marginal is: in the conditional \(x_t\) (hence \(\varepsilon\)) is given/fixed; in the marginal \(x_t\) is not given, so \(\varepsilon\) becomes random. Same draw formula \(x_{t-1}=A'x_0+c\varepsilon+\sigma_t z\), only "what's random" changes:

So what turns the conditional into the marginal is letting \(\varepsilon\) go random (averaging over \(x_t\)) — not the \(\sigma_t z\), which is present in both.

Where "input is correct" is used: "\(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\)" is the statement "\(x_t\) has marginal \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\)" (same distribution, since \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\)). We cash it in via \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)=1\) below.

C. Read the marginal and match. With \(x_0\) fixed, \(x_{t-1}=A'x_0+c\varepsilon+\sigma_t z\) is a constant plus two independent Gaussians, so it's Gaussian with mean \(A'x_0\) (noises average to \(0\)) and variance \(c^2+\sigma_t^2\) (independent variances add). The averaging happens right here — in these \(\mathbb E[\cdot]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}[\cdot]\):

$$\mathbb E[x_{t-1}\mid x_0]=A'x_0+c\underbrace{\mathbb E[\varepsilon]}_{0}+\sigma_t\underbrace{\mathbb E[z]}_{0}=A'x_0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}[x_{t-1}\mid x_0]=c^2\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}[\varepsilon]}_{1}+\sigma_t^2\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}[z]}_{1}=c^2+\sigma_t^2.$$

Concrete: if \(A'x_0{=}10,\ c{=}2,\ \sigma_t{=}3\), then \(x_{t-1}=10+2\varepsilon+3z\). As \(\varepsilon,z\) sweep \(\mathcal N(0,I)\): the \(+2\varepsilon\) and \(-2\varepsilon\) cases are equally likely so they cancel → mean \(=10\); the values scatter by \(2\varepsilon\) (variance \(4\)) plus \(3z\) (variance \(9\)) → variance \(=13=c^2+\sigma_t^2\). That sweep‑and‑average is the marginalization over \(x_t\).

Now demand this marginal equals the required \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)=\mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0,\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})I)\):

$$\text{mean: } A'x_0=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0\ \ (\forall x_0)\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{A'=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}},\qquad\qquad \text{variance: } c^2+\sigma_t^2=1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}\ \ (\text{Step 2}).$$

The mean half forces \(A'=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\) — one legal value, nothing from a hat. The variance half is one equation in the two remaining knobs \((c,\sigma_t)\): it ties \(c\) to \(\sigma_t\), leaving \(\sigma_t\) free — the family dial. Plugging \(A'\) back:

$$q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)=\mathcal N\!\Big(\underbrace{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0}_{\text{forced}}+\underbrace{c\,\varepsilon}_{\text{knob 1}},\ \ \underbrace{\sigma_t^2 I}_{\text{knob 2}}\Big).$$

Step 2 — force the marginals to match (this is "Gaussian marginalization")

Demand that this reverse step, fed the correct \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), reproduces the correct \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)=\mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})I)\). Sample the chain given \(x_0\): let \(\varepsilon,z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) independent, then

$$x_{t-1}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0+c\,\varepsilon+\sigma_t\,z.$$

The mean is already \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0\) — good. The two independent Gaussian terms add variances, so the total variance is \(c^2+\sigma_t^2\). Matching it to the required \((1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\) fixes \(c\):

$$c^2+\sigma_t^2=1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}\;\Longrightarrow\;c=\sqrt{\,1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2\,}.$$

That's the entire trick. One equation, one unknown eliminated — and \(\sigma_t\) survives as a free parameter for every member of the family. Substituting \(c\) back gives Song et al. (2021)'s reverse conditional:

$$\boxed{\;q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)=\mathcal N\!\Big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\varepsilon,\;\sigma_t^2 I\Big)\;}$$

Every \(\sigma_t\ge0\) gives a different forward process (most of them non‑Markovian — \(x_{t-1}\) now depends on \(x_0\) as well as \(x_t\)), yet all share the identical marginals. By Step 1 the DDPM weights are the right denoiser for the whole family. No retraining — you just pick \(\sigma_t\) at sampling time.

Step 3 — where the two ends of the family live

\(\sigma_t\) slides continuously between the two methods you already know:

  • \(\sigma_t=\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}\) (the DDPM posterior std) → the coefficients rearrange into exactly DDPM's posterior mean \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\) with variance \(\tilde\beta_t\), so the step is the DDPM ancestral posterior — fully stochastic. (Quick check: \(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\tilde\beta_t =\tfrac{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})^2}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\), so \(c=\tfrac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\), and \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0+c\varepsilon\) collapses to \(\tilde\mu_t\).)
  • \(\sigma_t=0\) → the step's own noise is gone: the conditional \(q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\) becomes a point mass at its mean (zero spread), so given \(x_t,x_0\) the next \(x_{t-1}\) is determined exactly. This is DDIM — deterministic. (The eta knob multiplies the DDPM \(\sigma_t\): eta=1 DDPM, eta=0 DDIM, in between a blend.)

Step 4 — the DDIM update (exactly what the code runs)

Set \(\sigma_t=0\), so \(c=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\). We don't know the true \(x_0\) or \(\varepsilon\), so plug in the network's estimates — \(\hat\varepsilon=\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\) and the implied clean image \(\hat x_0=(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\):

$$\boxed{\;x_{t-1}=\underbrace{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat x_0}_{\text{predicted clean image}}+\underbrace{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat\varepsilon}_{\text{re‑noise to level }t-1}\;}$$

Read literally: estimate the clean image, then re‑noise it to the next, lower noise level. Notice what the derivation never used: that \(t{-}1\) is adjacent to \(t\). The marginal‑matching argument only referenced the two noise levels' \(\bar\alpha\) values, so the same formula holds for any jump \(\bar\alpha_t\to\bar\alpha_s\), \(sskip timesteps — 20–50 steps instead of 1000. Step count is the speed knob, and it costs no retraining.

The whole story in one breath

Sampling needs \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) (②), which is intractable — so every method here instead uses the exact posterior \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\) (①) with the network's \(\hat x_0\) plugged in. But ① isn't unique: there's a one‑parameter family of exact posteriors — indexed by \(\sigma_t\) — that all share the marginals the loss was trained on, so the same weights fit every one. DDPM is the \(\sigma_t=\text{(posterior std)}\) member; DDIM is simply the \(\sigma_t=0\) member. Zero noise makes the step deterministic, and because the marginal argument never needed adjacent timesteps, you can also take big jumps. That is the entire permission slip: same target, same weights, different (legal) choice of \(\sigma_t\).

The deepest reason: it's an ODE

In the continuous limit the deterministic update becomes the probability‑flow ODE

$$\frac{dx}{dt}=f(t)\,x-\tfrac12 g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x),$$

a theorem‑backed ODE with the same marginals \(p_t\) as the stochastic reverse at every time. DDIM is a first‑order discretization of it. Determinism (fixed seed → fixed image; smooth latent interpolation) is a free bonus of dropping the injected noise, not a quality tax.

3Score matching & why Langevin converges to \(p(x)\)

The score, and why we learn it

The score \(s(x)=\nabla_x\log p(x)\) is a vector field pointing toward higher density. Learning it dodges the intractable normalizer \(Z\) in \(p=\tilde p/Z\), because \(\nabla\log p=\nabla\log\tilde p\) (the constant differentiates away).

We can't regress the true score, but denoising score matching gives an equivalent target: the score of the noised density is closed‑form, \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t=-\varepsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\). Hence:

The punchline that ties §3 back to §1

Training to predict \(\varepsilon\) (DDPM) is denoising score matching. The DDPM net, divided by \(-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\), is a score estimate \(\hat s(x_t,t)\). You never trained a separate "score model" — you already have one.

Langevin dynamics: sampling from a score

Given \(s(x)\), draw samples with

$$x_{k+1}=x_k+\tfrac{\eta}{2}\,\nabla_x\log p(x_k)+\sqrt{\eta}\,z_k,\qquad z_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I).$$

Gradient ascent on log‑density (climb toward likely regions) plus calibrated noise (so you spread out to match the true distribution instead of collapsing to the single peak).

Why it converges — the proof, at the right depth

This is the Euler–Maruyama discretization of the Langevin SDE \(dx=\tfrac12\nabla\log p\,dt+dW_t\). Its density \(\rho_t\) evolves by the Fokker–Planck equation:

$$\frac{\partial\rho_t}{\partial t}=-\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\tfrac12\rho_t\nabla\log p\big)+\tfrac12\nabla^2\rho_t.$$

One‑line proof that \(p\) is stationary. Substitute \(\rho_t=p\) and use \(p\,\nabla\log p=\nabla p\):

$$-\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\tfrac12 p\,\nabla\log p\big)+\tfrac12\nabla^2p=-\tfrac12\nabla^2p+\tfrac12\nabla^2p=0.$$

So \(\partial_t\rho_t=0\) at \(\rho_t=p\): once the chain reaches \(p\) it stays there. A second, harder result (ergodicity / a log‑Sobolev inequality) says it reaches that fixed point from any start, exponentially fast — that existence‑and‑uniqueness part is the genuinely technical piece.

Should you dwell on it?

No. Do the five‑line stationarity check once (above) so Langevin feels inevitable, then move on. The rate proofs (log‑Sobolev constants, mixing times) are a functional‑analysis rabbit hole that doesn't pay off for building models — because modern diffusion barely uses pure Langevin. Early NCSN needed annealed Langevin (a score is only accurate near data), and DDPM/DDIM/flow replaced that with a noise schedule + a single reverse pass, which is far more stable. Know the score↔noise identity cold; know Langevin's stationary distribution is \(p\); skip the mixing‑time analysis.

4EDM — the design space that unifies DDPM, DDIM, score & flow

EDM (Karras et al., 2022, "Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion Models") is not a new idea so much as a cleanup: it rewrites all of the above in one continuous‑noise‑level language, then tunes each knob independently. This is what DIAMOND uses — so it's directly on your path.

4.1 Drop the timestep index; parametrize by noise level \(\sigma\) directly

Instead of \(t\in\{0,\dots,T\}\) and \(\bar\alpha_t\), EDM writes the noisy sample as

$$x=x_0+\sigma\,\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),$$

with \(\sigma\) ranging continuously from \(\sigma_{\min}\) (almost clean) to \(\sigma_{\max}\) (almost pure noise). DDPM's schedule is just one path through \(\sigma\)‑space; treating \(\sigma\) as the fundamental variable removes the arbitrary "1000 steps" and lets sampling and training be designed separately.

4.2 Preconditioning — the part that actually raises quality

Rather than have the raw network \(F_\theta\) predict noise, EDM wraps it so the network's job is well‑scaled at every \(\sigma\). The denoiser \(D_\theta\) (which outputs the clean \(x_0\)) is:

$$D_\theta(x,\sigma)=c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)\,x+c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)\,F_\theta\big(c_{\text{in}}(\sigma)\,x,\;c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma)\big).$$
  • \(c_{\text{in}}\) scales the input so it has unit variance whatever \(\sigma\) is.
  • \(c_{\text{skip}}\) lets the net predict a correction to \(x\): at low noise it should mostly copy the input (denoising is easy); at high noise it must predict almost everything. The blend is \(\sigma\)‑dependent.
  • \(c_{\text{out}}\) scales the target so the regression target has unit variance — so the loss is balanced across noise levels and no single \(\sigma\) dominates the gradient.
  • \(c_{\text{noise}}\) maps \(\sigma\) to the conditioning input (like a timestep embedding).

All four are derived from one number, the data scale \(\sigma_{\text{data}}\), so that the network's input and output are unit‑variance at every noise level. That single normalization — not a new objective — is most of EDM's FID win. It also picks a sensible loss weighting \(\lambda(\sigma)\) and samples training \(\sigma\) from a log‑normal, so mid‑range noise levels (where most of the learning is) get the most attention.

4.3 The Karras noise schedule and the Heun sampler

Schedule (sampling‑time): the \(\sigma\) values you visit are spaced by a power law with parameter \(\rho\):

$$\sigma_i=\Big(\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho}+\tfrac{i}{N-1}\big(\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho}-\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho}\big)\Big)^{\rho},\quad \rho\approx7.$$

This puts more steps at low noise (where detail is decided) than a naive uniform schedule — a big quality‑per‑step gain.

Sampler: a 2nd‑order Heun integrator of the probability‑flow ODE (predict with Euler, then correct with the average slope). Two net calls per step, but so accurate that ~15–35 steps rival 1000‑step DDPM. Optional stochasticity (\(S_{\text{churn}}\): add a little noise then denoise) recovers some of the error‑correcting benefit of the SDE when you want it.

5Flow matching — the full derivation

The one you wanted in most depth: starting conditions, the vector field, the continuity equation, where divergence enters, the "do it for one point then generalize" trick, the \(x_0{-}x_1\) pairing, and the known issue. Built from nothing.

Convention (read this first)

Flow‑matching papers put noise at \(t{=}0\) and data at \(t{=}1\) and integrate forward in \(t\). That's the opposite of the DDPM index in §1–4. We follow the flow convention here (and it's exactly what the repo's core/flow_matching.py does).

5.1 The primitives (your two ingredients)

Pick a probability path \(p_t(x)\) that morphs simple noise \(p_0=\mathcal N(0,I)\) into data \(p_1=p_{\text{data}}\), and learn a velocity (vector) field \(u_t(x)\) — the course's symbol; some papers write \(v\) — that transports samples along it. Four objects, in order:

  • trajectory \(x_t\): one sample's path over time;
  • flow \(\psi_t(x_0)\): the whole bundle of trajectories from every start \(x_0\);
  • probability path \(p_t(x)\): the distribution of \(x_t\) at time \(t\);
  • velocity field \(u_t(x)\): "where to move — direction and speed — at time \(t\), location \(x\)."

Generation = draw \(x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) and integrate the ODE forward:

$$\frac{dx_t}{dt}=u_t(x_t),\qquad \hat x_1=x_0+\int_0^1 u_t^\theta(x_t)\,dt.$$
🛣 Highway vs 🧭 Compass — velocity vs score

The course's sharpest intuition: the velocity \(u_t(x)\) is a highway — it tells you the exact road, direction and speed to travel. The score \(\nabla_x\log p\) is only a compass — it points toward higher density but not how far or fast. Flow matching learns the highway directly, which is why a plain ODE step follows it so cleanly.

The two things "needed": boundary conditions (\(p_0=\)noise, \(p_1=\)data) and a field that "obeys the continuity equation" — the constraint tying \(u_t\) to \(p_t\), next. When they hold, we say \(u_t\) generates \(p_t\): if \(x_0\sim p_0\) and \(\dot x_t=u_t(x_t)\), then \(x_t\sim p_t\) for all \(t\).

5.2 The continuity equation — and where divergence enters

The course builds this from a "mass conservation" fluid picture: draw a little box; the rate the density inside changes equals inflow − outflow. If probability mass is only moved by \(u_t\) (never created/destroyed), the density must satisfy the continuity (transport) equation:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t}(x)=-\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(p_t\,u_t\big)(x)\;}$$

This is the only place divergence \(\nabla\cdot\) is conceptually needed — so first, what it is. The divergence of a vector field at a point is a single number: the net rate stuff flows out of a tiny box around that point (outflow − inflow, per unit volume). Positive ⇒ a source (net leaving); negative ⇒ a sink (net arriving).

The subtlety the course flags with a big ❌/✅: the divergence is of the flux \(p_t\,u_t\), not the bare velocity \(u_t\). Here is why, physically. What actually moves is probability mass, and the amount crossing a point per second is how much is there × how fast it's going \(=p_t\,u_t\) — the "mass current." Velocity alone gives the speed but says nothing about how much mass is riding it; only the flux \(p_t u_t\) is the quantity that's conserved and bookkept. So read the boxed equation in words: density change at a point = minus the net flux out of it — more mass leaving than arriving ⇒ \(p_t\) there drops (that's the minus sign). A pair \((p_t,u_t)\) obeying it is compatible, and then \(u_t\) generates \(p_t\). Hold onto "flux, not velocity" — it is the through‑line that makes the whole averaging trick in 5.3 work.

(Divergence returns if you want exact log‑likelihoods: \(\tfrac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t)=-\nabla\!\cdot\!u_t(x_t)\) — note here it's the bare velocity, because now you're tracking one moving sample's density, not a mass balance over space. Useful for evaluation, not for training or sampling.)

Proof it actually works — the moving samples' density really does obey this equation

We didn't assume the boxed equation; it is forced the moment you push samples along the ODE. One idea — "probe with a test function" — makes it rigorous in four lines, and it proves both halves at once: that the equation holds, and that "\(u_t\) generates \(p_t\)."

Setup. Start \(x_0\sim p_0\) and move every sample by \(\dot x_t=u_t(x_t)\). Let \(p_t\) be the resulting density of \(x_t\) (its "pushforward"). Claim: \(p_t\) must satisfy \(\partial_t p_t=-\nabla\!\cdot(p_t u_t)\).

The trick — track the average of an arbitrary smooth "probe" \(\varphi\) in two ways, and equate.

Way 1 — move the sample (chain rule + the ODE \(\dot x_t=u_t\)):

$$\frac{d}{dt}\,\mathbb E[\varphi(x_t)]=\mathbb E\big[\nabla\varphi(x_t)\cdot\dot x_t\big]=\mathbb E\big[\nabla\varphi(x_t)\cdot u_t(x_t)\big]=\int \nabla\varphi\cdot u_t\,p_t\,dx.$$

Way 2 — differentiate the density (since \(\mathbb E[\varphi(x_t)]=\int\varphi\,p_t\,dx\)):

$$\frac{d}{dt}\,\mathbb E[\varphi(x_t)]=\int \varphi\,\partial_t p_t\,dx.$$

Equate the two, then integrate by parts (the boundary term vanishes — \(\varphi\) decays / has compact support):

$$\int \varphi\,\partial_t p_t\,dx=\int \nabla\varphi\cdot u_t\,p_t\,dx=-\int \varphi\,\nabla\!\cdot(p_t u_t)\,dx.$$

This holds for every probe \(\varphi\). Two functions that integrate the same against all \(\varphi\) must be equal, so the integrands match:

$$\boxed{\;\partial_t p_t=-\,\nabla\!\cdot(p_t u_t)\;}$$

"Probe against every \(\varphi\)" is exactly how you turn "samples move by \(u\)" into a statement about the density. And notice the flux \(p_t u_t\) appeared on its own, from integrating \(\nabla\varphi\cdot u_t\,p_t\) by parts — that is precisely why the divergence lands on the flux, not the bare velocity. (Physics gives the same law faster: mass in a fixed box changes only by what crosses its walls, \(\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega p=-\oint_{\partial\Omega}p\,u\cdot n\), and the divergence theorem rewrites the wall‑flux as \(\int_\Omega\nabla\!\cdot(pu)\).)

5.3 The problem, and the one‑sample cheat

Building \(v_t\) for the full data distribution directly needs an intractable integral. The trick you half‑remembered — do it for one point, then generalize.

Step A — one data point is trivial (the conditional path)

Condition on a single data sample \(x_1\). Define a conditional probability path — a Gaussian that starts wide and collapses onto \(x_1\) (this specific choice is rectified flow, what SD3/Flux use and what this repo implements):

$$p_t(x\mid x_1)=\mathcal N\big(t\,x_1,\;(1-t)^2 I\big)\quad\Rightarrow\quad t{=}0:\ \mathcal N(0,I)=p_0,\ \ t{=}1:\ \delta_{x_1}.$$

Its trajectory is the straight interpolation \(x_t=(1-t)\,x_0+t\,x_1\) with \(x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\). The conditional velocity that generates it is (course's form, then simplified using the interpolation):

$$u_t(x\mid x_1)=\frac{x_1-x}{1-t}\;\xrightarrow{\ x=x_t\ }\;\boxed{\;u_t(x_t\mid x_1)=x_1-x_0\;}$$

Why that cancellation. Substitute the interpolation \(x=x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1\) into \((x_1-x)/(1-t)\): the numerator becomes \(x_1-x_t=x_1-(1-t)x_0-t x_1=(1-t)(x_1-x_0)\), so the \((1-t)\) cancels top and bottom, leaving \(x_1-x_0\). Two readings of the same field: the left form \((x_1-x)/(1-t)\) is the velocity at any position \(x\) (what the ODE and the continuity check need — a field over all of space); the right form \(x_1-x_0\) is its value along this pair's own straight line (a single constant vector — what the loss will regress).

This is your "\(x_0{-}x_1\) is different each time." Per pair the target velocity is just the displacement from the noise sample to the data sample — constant along the line, nothing to learn per pair.

Step B — the marginalization theorem (the "generalize to all points" magic)

Average the conditional paths over the data to get the marginal path, and average the conditional fields — weighted by the posterior \(p(x_1\mid x)\) — to get the marginal field:

$$p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid x_1)\,p_{\text{data}}(x_1)\,dx_1,\qquad u_t(x)=\int u_t(x\mid x_1)\,\underbrace{\frac{p_t(x\mid x_1)\,p_{\text{data}}(x_1)}{p_t(x)}}_{=\,p(x_1\mid x)}\,dx_1 =\mathbb E\big[u_t(x\mid x_1)\mid x_t{=}x\big].$$

Why weighted by the posterior \(p(x_1\mid x)\), and not a plain average? A single location \(x\) sits on many straight lines — every \((x_0,x_1)\) pair whose interpolant happens to pass through \(x\). Each one wants to move with its own velocity \(x_1-x_0\), and they disagree. The right marginal velocity at \(x\) is the average of those, but weighted by how plausible each destination \(x_1\) is given you actually landed at \(x\) — that's the posterior \(p(x_1\mid x)\); a data point that rarely routes through \(x\) barely gets a vote. And that exact weighting isn't a modelling choice — it's forced by 5.2: what adds across the pairs is flux (the mass currents \(p_t u_t\)), not velocity. So you sum the conditional fluxes and divide by the total density \(p_t(x)\) — and "\(\sum\)flux \(/\) density" is the posterior‑weighted mean written above. (There's "flux, not velocity" paying off.)

So the marginal velocity is a posterior mean: "given where you are, where should you go?" Theorem (Lipman 2022 / Liu 2022): if each conditional pair satisfies the continuity equation, this average \((p_t,u_t)\) does too — and \(p_t\) interpolates exactly noise→data. The simple per‑sample fields, averaged, are the true global transport — without ever computing the intractable \(p_t\).

Is that always true — do the conditional and the marginal both satisfy continuity every time? Yes, and it's no coincidence: it's pure linearity (full proof in the pink box below). Given only that each conditional pair obeys continuity — plus mild regularity to swap \(\partial_t,\nabla\cdot\) with the integral — the marginal pair must too. Nothing here is special to Gaussians or straight lines; those are just our convenient choice of conditional path. The one thing you must get right is the averaging: sum the flux and divide by density (the posterior‑weighted mean). Naively averaging the velocities would give a different, wrong field. So — to your question — what was "discovered" in 2022 is not this identity (it's old mass‑conservation math); it's that the identity lets you train the field almost for free. See the "why only 2022?" box after 5.3.

Why the averaged field satisfies continuity — general, and it applies to our case

The argument is general (it holds for any conditional path+velocity that individually satisfies continuity), and our rectified‑flow pair is exactly such a case, so it applies directly. Three steps, with our specific pair plugged in.

Step 1 — each conditional satisfies continuity. "The velocity generates the path" means: for each fixed \(x_1\),

$$\partial_t\, p_t(x\mid x_1)=-\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(p_t(x\mid x_1)\,u_t(x\mid x_1)\big),\qquad\text{ours: }\ p_t(\cdot\mid x_1)=\mathcal N(tx_1,(1-t)^2I),\ \ u_t(x\mid x_1)=x_1-x_0.$$

Why "ours does" is free. Our conditional velocity \(u_t(x_t\mid x_1)=x_1-x_0\) is literally the time‑derivative \(\dot x_t\) of the moving sample \(x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1\). So the proof just above ("probe with \(\varphi\)") applies verbatim at each fixed \(x_1\) — no new algebra needed. If you'd rather see the PDE ground out by hand, here it is:

Brute‑force check (1‑D, fully concrete). Fix \(x_1\); write \(m=t x_1\), \(\sigma=1-t\), so \(p(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}e^{-(x-m)^2/2\sigma^2}\) and \(u=\dfrac{x_1-x}{\sigma}\). Differentiate each side (using \(\dot m=x_1,\ \dot\sigma=-1\)):

$$\partial_t p=p\Big[\tfrac1\sigma+\tfrac{(x-m)\,x_1}{\sigma^2}-\tfrac{(x-m)^2}{\sigma^3}\Big],\qquad -\partial_x(p\,u)=p\Big[\tfrac1\sigma+\tfrac{(x-m)(x_1-x)}{\sigma^3}\Big].$$

Their difference is \(\dfrac{p\,(x-m)}{\sigma^3}\big[x_1\sigma-(x-m)-(x_1-x)\big]\), and the bracket vanishes: \(x_1(1-t)-(x-tx_1)-(x_1-x)=0\). So \(\partial_t p=-\partial_x(p\,u)\) exactly — our Gaussian conditional obeys continuity, confirmed by hand.

Step 2 — average over \(x_1\); the \(\partial_t\) and \(\nabla\cdot\) pass straight through the integral (both are linear operators):

$$p_t(x)=\!\int p_t(x\mid x_1)p_{\text{data}}(x_1)\,dx_1\ \Rightarrow\ \partial_t p_t(x)=\!\int \partial_t p_t(x\mid x_1)\,p_{\text{data}}\,dx_1=-\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\underbrace{\int p_t(x\mid x_1)\,u_t(x\mid x_1)\,p_{\text{data}}\,dx_1}_{\text{the marginal flux}}.$$

Step 3 — name that flux \(p_t(x)\,u_t(x)\), which defines the marginal velocity:

$$u_t(x)=\frac{1}{p_t(x)}\!\int u_t(x\mid x_1)\,p_t(x\mid x_1)p_{\text{data}}(x_1)\,dx_1=\mathbb E\big[u_t(x\mid x_1)\mid x_t{=}x\big]\ \overset{\text{ours}}{=}\ \mathbb E\big[x_1-x_0\mid x_t{=}x\big].$$

Substituting back gives \(\partial_t p_t=-\nabla\!\cdot(p_t u_t)\): the marginal pair satisfies continuity. So for our rectified flow the true transport field is exactly \(u_t(x)=\mathbb E[x_1-x_0\mid x_t{=}x]\) — and the CFM loss (5.4), being MSE onto \(x_1-x_0\), learns precisely that conditional expectation. The trained \(u_\theta^\star\) is the continuity‑satisfying field, in our case, not just in the abstract.

Same shape as §1: there the reverse mean was linear in \(x_0\), so averaging conditional posteriors landed on the true marginal mean; here the flux \(p_t u_t\) is linear, so averaging conditional fields lands on the true transport. Linear operator + MSE‑gives‑conditional‑mean = the averaged target is the real target.

If the math is this old, why is flow matching from 2022?

Fair confusion — Euler wrote the continuity equation in 1757, and ODEs, Gaussians, and the divergence theorem are centuries old. Nothing above is new mathematics. What was missing for 250 years was a way to train the velocity field cheaply.

  • 2018 — the idea existed but was slow. Continuous Normalizing Flows (Neural ODE / FFJORD) already generated data by integrating an ODE whose density obeys continuity. But they trained by maximum likelihood, which needs the divergence term \(\nabla\!\cdot u\) (a trace/Jacobian) and a full ODE solve inside every gradient step. Expensive, memory‑hungry, unstable — it never scaled. That wall stood for four years.
  • 2020–21 — diffusion primed the ground. DDPM and score‑based SDEs proved noise→data generation works at scale, and the probability‑flow ODE (Song 2021) showed a deterministic ODE with the right marginals exists. Crucially they popularised the exact trick being transplanted: an intractable target that is a conditional expectation can be learned by regressing the freely‑sampled conditional — the same identity behind "predict the noise" in §1.
  • 2022 — the unlock: simulation‑free training. Lipman/Liu/Albergo saw you can regress the network onto the conditional velocity \(x_1-x_0\) (free to sample), which has the same gradient as the intractable marginal loss (5.4). No ODE in the loop, no divergence, no determinant — just plain MSE on billions of \((x_0,x_1)\) pairs. Plus the freedom to pick straight‑line paths, which sample in far fewer steps.

So the obstacle was never the continuity equation — it was "how do you fit \(u\) without integrating the ODE and computing a Jacobian every step." That answer, not the math, is what arrived in 2022. Flow matching is a training recipe, and it rode the diffusion wave (big nets, big compute, proof that noise→data works) to become practical.

5.4 The loss — why regressing the conditional field learns the marginal one

The ideal Flow‑Matching loss \(\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}=\mathbb E\lVert u_t^\theta(x)-u_t(x)\rVert^2\) is intractable (\(u_t\) is that integral). But we can sample the conditional target \(x_1-x_0\) for free, giving the tractable Conditional Flow Matching loss:

$$\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}=\mathbb E_{t,x_1,x}\Big[\big\lVert u_t^\theta\big((1-t)x_0+t x_1,\,t\big)-\underbrace{(x_1-x_0)}_{u_t(x\mid x_1)}\big\rVert^2\Big].$$
The load‑bearing fact the course states

\(\;\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}\;\) — the two losses have the same gradient (expand the squared norm and use the definition of the marginal). So minimizing the tractable CFM loss trains the exact same field as the intractable FM loss.

Why, in one line — the same identity that makes DDPM's noise‑prediction work: the L2 minimizer is the conditional expectation of the target. The net sees only \(x=x_t\) (many pairs land there), so the loss forces

$$u_\theta^\star(x,t)=\mathbb E\big[x_1-x_0\mid x_t=x\big]=u_t(x),$$

exactly the marginal field from Step B. Minimizing the tractable per‑pair loss yields the intractable global velocity — structurally identical to why "predict the noise" recovers the score.

5.6 The issue you heard about

Crossing paths → curved marginal field → more steps

The conditional paths are straight, but two different pairs can route through the same point \(x\) at time \(t\) heading different directions. There the marginal field \(u_t(x)=\mathbb E[x_1-x_0\mid x_t=x]\) is the average of conflicting velocities — so the actual learned trajectory \(u_t^\theta\) is non‑linear and bends, even though every target was straight. Curved paths need more ODE steps ("no magic solver saves the day"), undercutting the few‑step promise. The course's fix:

  • Reflow / rectification (Liu 2022, "Flow Straight and Fast"): the model you just trained is the "1‑rectified flow." Now generate paired data by simulating it — \(z_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), \(z_1=\psi_1^\theta(z_0)\) — so each noise is paired with the data it actually flows to (not a random one), and retrain on \((z_0,z_1)\) to get the "2‑rectified flow." Repeat.
  • (Adjacent idea, not in the deck: minibatch optimal‑transport coupling pairs \(x_0{\leftrightarrow}x_1\) to minimize transport cost, achieving similar straightening in one shot.)

Why reflow works — two proofs the course sketches: (1) distribution preserved — \(X\) and the reflowed \(Z\) satisfy the same continuity equation, so by uniqueness \(p_t^X=p_t^Z\) for all \(t\) (you still sample \(p_{\text{data}}\)); (2) paths straighten — a straightness measure \(S(Z^k)\) telescopes to \(\min_{k\le K}S(Z^k)\le O(1/K)\). In practice 1–2 reflows, then you can afford plain Euler at inference. Root cause, crisply: independent noise↔data coupling creates the crossings; a model‑induced (or OT) coupling removes them.

5.7 Why flow matching is winning now (vs DDPM / DDIM / EDM)

SD3, Flux, and most 2024–25 models train with flow matching (usually the rectified / linear‑interpolant form). It's not that the others are "wrong" — they're all the same generative‑ODE family (see §4, §5.8) — but flow matching is the most convenient point in that space. Concretely:

  • Straighter trajectories ⇒ fewer steps. The whole game at inference is "how many network evaluations (NFEs) to integrate the ODE accurately," and that count is set by how much the path bends: Euler's error per step is \(\approx\tfrac12\Delta t^2\,\ddot x\) — proportional to curvature — so a straight, constant‑speed path (\(\ddot x=0\)) is integrated exactly in one step, while a curved one needs many small ones.
    What "conditional path" means, and why FM's is straight. Fix one noise \(x_0\) and one data point \(x_1\); the conditional path is the route the interpolation draws between those two fixed endpoints. Every method writes it as \((\text{noise coeff})\,x_0+(\text{signal coeff})\,x_1\) — they differ only in which route the coefficients take from "all noise" \((1,0)\) to "all data" \((0,1)\):
    • Flow matching: coefficients \((1-t,\,t)\) — the straight diagonal. So \(x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1\) is a straight line at constant velocity \(x_1-x_0\): zero curvature.
    • DDPM / VP: coefficients \(\big(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t},\,\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\big)\) obey \(a^2+b^2=1\), so they ride the quarter‑circle from \((1,0)\) to \((0,1)\). Same endpoints, but the arc — the point sweeps a curved route, its velocity always turning.
    • EDM / VE: \(x_t=x_1+\sigma_t\varepsilon\) with a very non‑linear \(\sigma_t\) (Karras \(\rho{=}7\)); the direction is fixed but the speed \(\dot\sigma_t\) swings enormously and the marginal ODE curves sharply near the data — exactly why EDM needs 2nd‑order Heun and a hand‑tuned \(\sigma\)-spacing to keep the step count down.
    Same two endpoints, but DDPM takes the circular arc and flow matching the straight chord. Because FM's conditional targets are straight and constant‑speed, the learned marginal field stays close to straight, so plain Euler with ~10–30 steps (1–4 after distillation) tracks it; the arc/curved schedules bend the sampling ODE and cost steps. "More curved" is precisely the property that hurts DDPM/EDM here.
    The "sin/cos" schedule you heard about — concretely

    That's the cosine schedule (Improved DDPM, Nichol–Dhariwal 2021), and it is the arc. Let an angle \(\theta_t\) sweep \(0\to\tfrac\pi2\) and set

    $$\bar\alpha_t=\cos^2\theta_t\ \Rightarrow\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}=\cos\theta_t,\ \ \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}=\sin\theta_t,\qquad x_t=\cos\theta_t\,x_1+\sin\theta_t\,\varepsilon.$$

    The coefficient pair \((\cos\theta_t,\sin\theta_t)\) is a point on the unit circle, sweeping the quarter‑arc — that is exactly why the VP/DDPM conditional path curves: it's steered by a rotating angle. (Original DDPM used a linear \(\beta_t\); cosine replaced it because linear wiped out the signal too fast near the end — but it's still an arc.) DDIM is only a sampler, so it inherits whatever schedule — usually cosine — the model was trained with.

    EDM isn't sin/cos — it's variance‑exploding: \(x=x_1+\sigma\varepsilon\), with \(\sigma\) from \(\sim0.002\) to \(\sim80\) on a power‑law grid \(\sigma_i\propto(\cdot)^{\rho},\ \rho{=}7\). A different curve, same consequence: non‑uniform speed ⇒ curved sampling ODE ⇒ Heun + tuned spacing.

    Flow matching drops the trig entirely: coefficients \((1-t,\,t)\) — the straight chord from \((1,0)\) to \((0,1)\), constant velocity \(x_1-x_0\). (You could build a cosine‑path flow — Lipman's framework allows any Gaussian path — but rectified flow / SD3 / Flux deliberately pick the straight line. That choice is the point.)

  • Simpler, schedule‑free objective. Diffusion forces a stack of coupled choices, each affecting stability and quality: a variance schedule \(\beta_t\) (linear? cosine?), an ELBO to derive, what the net predicts (\(\varepsilon\) vs \(x_0\) vs \(v\)), and a per‑timestep loss weighting. Flow matching collapses all of it to one recipe: sample \(t\sim U(0,1)\), interpolate \(x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1\), regress the velocity onto \(x_1-x_0\). No \(\bar\alpha_t\) schedule, no ELBO, no noise‑vs‑\(x_0\) debate — one target (the displacement), and it behaves the same at every \(t\). Fewer things to tune or get wrong.
  • Well‑behaved target. The velocity target \(x_1-x_0\) has roughly unit scale across all \(t\); no blow‑ups like DDPM's \(1/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\) at high noise (the §1 "scar"). Training is more stable.
  • Continuous‑time & solver‑agnostic. It's an ODE from the start (no "drop the noise term" step), so any off‑the‑shelf ODE solver (Euler, Heun, DPM‑Solver) plugs in, and time is genuinely continuous.
  • EDM already got ~80% of the way. EDM's insight (preconditioning, \(\sigma\)-space, Heun) made diffusion far more efficient; flow matching keeps EDM's good ideas but drops the curved schedule for a straight one — so it's the natural successor, not a break.
Flow matching's real issues (don't oversell it)

Each issue below as what · why · impact.

  • The marginal field is still curved — so "1 step" isn't free. What: even vanilla FM's sampling paths bend. Why: straight conditional targets average into a bent marginal field wherever training lines cross (5.6). Impact: you still need a handful of Euler steps (~10–30); genuine 1‑step needs straightening (reflow) or distillation (§12) — the linear interpolant buys "few," not "one."
  • Reflow trades straightness for fidelity. What: each reflow round straightens paths but erodes sample quality. Why: reflow retrains on the model's own generated pairs \((x_0,\text{Flow}(x_0))\), so any error in the current model is baked into the next round's training data — errors compound and fine detail/sharpness drifts. Impact: 1–2 reflows is the sweet spot; "perfectly straight" and "high fidelity" pull against each other, so people stop early.
  • The high‑noise end (= the start of sampling) is still hard. What: near \(t{\approx}0\) you're at almost pure noise — the very first steps of generation. Why: a near‑noise \(x_t\) is consistent with a huge set of possible final images, so the per‑pair target \(x_1-x_0\) swings wildly across the many pairs landing at the same \(x_t\); the net can only learn their average \(\mathbb E[x_1-x_0\mid x_t]\), which points at the data's "center of mass" in general — a blurry, non‑committal direction. The trajectory only commits to a specific image later, as noise drops and the posterior over \(x_1\) narrows (the same "decide gradually" story as §1). Impact: this region is the hardest to learn (mushy, high‑variance target) and the most error‑sensitive — which is exactly why modern training samples \(t\) from a logit‑normal (§10) to spend capacity there, rather than uniformly. It's the same "posterior is wide at high noise" wall diffusion hits.
  • The coupling (which noise pairs with which data) matters. What: the pairing sets the curvature. Why: random independent pairing makes lines cross a lot → curvature (5.6). How the fix works — minibatch‑OT: inside each training minibatch of \(N\) noise and \(N\) data samples, solve a small assignment problem that pairs them to minimize total \(\sum\lVert x_0-x_1\rVert^2\); an optimal (non‑crossing) assignment means the straight targets barely cross, straightening the field in one shot (no iterating like reflow). Impact: straighter paths / fewer steps without reflow's fidelity loss — cost is an assignment solve per minibatch (Hungarian, or an approximate Sinkhorn step), a modest overhead.
  • Guidance & exact likelihood need extra care. What: two things that are clean in diffusion cost more here. Why — CFG: guidance is applied to the velocity \(u_\varnothing+w(u_y-u_\varnothing)\); it works, but the geometry differs slightly from score‑CFG, so the scale \(w\) needs retuning. Why — likelihood: an exact log‑likelihood needs \(\int\nabla\!\cdot u\,dt\) along the ODE (the 5.2 footnote) — a trace/Jacobian per step (or a stochastic Hutchinson estimate), which is expensive. Impact: FM isn't a drop‑in for every diffusion recipe; guidance tuning and exact‑likelihood eval both cost more.

6Guidance — steering toward a class, text, or image

Everything so far samples the unconditional \(p(x)\). Guidance samples a conditional \(p(x\mid y)\) where \(y\) is a class, prompt, or (in practice) a CLIP embedding. This section follows the derivation as taught in the course (Dhariwal 2021 for classifier guidance; Ho & Salimans 2022 for classifier‑free). Notation: condition \(y\), guidance scale \(w\), generation weights \(\theta\), classifier weights \(\phi\), null token \(\varnothing\).

6.1 Classifier guidance — the score decomposition, derived

Goal: sample the conditional reverse step \(p(x_t\mid x_{t+1},y)\) (here \(x_{t+1}\) is the noisier step, \(x_t\) the cleaner one we produce), reusing a diffusion model you already have plus a small classifier. Every line is shown — nothing assumed clear.

Step 1 — Bayes on the reverse step, in full. Start from the definition of a conditional and split the joint the other way:

$$p(x_t\mid x_{t+1},y)=\frac{p(x_t,y\mid x_{t+1})}{p(y\mid x_{t+1})}=\frac{p(y\mid x_t,x_{t+1})\,p(x_t\mid x_{t+1})}{p(y\mid x_{t+1})}.$$

Two simplifications turn this into something you can compute:

  • The denominator is constant in \(x_t\). \(p(y\mid x_{t+1})\) contains no \(x_t\), so as a function of \(x_t\) it's just a normaliser — absorb it into "\(\propto\)".
  • \(p(y\mid x_t,x_{t+1})=p(y\mid x_t)\). \(x_{t+1}\) is only a noisier copy of \(x_t\); once you know the cleaner \(x_t\), the noisier \(x_{t+1}\) adds no information about the label (data‑processing / Markov). So the label reads the cleaner image only.
$$\boxed{\;p(x_t\mid x_{t+1},y)\ \propto\ \underbrace{p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t+1})}_{\text{diffusion model (generator)}}\cdot\underbrace{p_\phi(y\mid x_t)}_{\text{classifier on noisy }x_t}\;}$$

Step 2 — the same fact as a score identity (take \(\log\), then \(\nabla_{x_t}\)). Write Bayes in log form: \(\log p(x_t\mid x_{t+1},y)=\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t+1})+\log p_\phi(y\mid x_t)-\log p(y\mid x_{t+1})\). The last term has no \(x_t\), so \(\nabla_{x_t}\) kills it:

$$\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid x_{t+1},y)=\underbrace{\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t+1})}_{\text{unconditional score}}+\underbrace{\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(y\mid x_t)}_{\text{classifier gradient}}.$$

That is the whole idea in one line: conditional score = unconditional score + classifier gradient. Steps 3–4 just turn it into the concrete Gaussian update the sampler runs.

Step 3 — write the two log‑terms explicitly. Abbreviate the generator's mean \(\mu:=\mu_\theta(x_{t+1})\) and variance \(\sigma^2:=\sigma_{t+1}^2\).

  • Generator — the DDPM Gaussian from §1: \(\displaystyle \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t+1})=-\frac{\lVert x_t-\mu\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}+\text{const}.\)
  • Classifier — no closed form, but the generator Gaussian is sharply peaked at \(\mu\), so we only need \(\log p_\phi\) near \(\mu\). Taylor‑expand to first order about \(x_t=\mu\):
    $$\log p_\phi(y\mid x_t)\approx \log p_\phi(y\mid\mu)+(x_t-\mu)^\top g,\qquad g:=\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(y\mid x_t)\big|_{x_t=\mu}.$$
    (A constant \(+\) a linear ramp — the same "thin peak sees only a straight ramp of the other factor" move as §1's spotlight.)

Step 4 — add the logs and complete the square (no skips). Let \(u:=x_t-\mu\). Add the two logs:

$$\log\big[p_\theta\,p_\phi\big]=-\frac{\lVert u\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}+u^\top g+\text{const}.$$

Factor \(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\) out of the \(u\)-terms, then complete the square using \(\lVert u\rVert^2-2\sigma^2 u^\top g=\lVert u-\sigma^2 g\rVert^2-\sigma^4\lVert g\rVert^2\):

$$-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\big(\lVert u\rVert^2-2\sigma^2 u^\top g\big)=-\frac{\lVert u-\sigma^2 g\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}+\underbrace{\frac{\sigma^2\lVert g\rVert^2}{2}}_{\text{no }u\ \Rightarrow\ \text{into const}}.$$

So the log is \(-\lVert u-\sigma^2 g\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)+\text{const}\) — a Gaussian in \(u=x_t-\mu\) centered at \(\sigma^2 g\). Undo the shift (\(x_t=\mu+u\)):

$$\boxed{\;x_t\ \sim\ \mathcal N\!\Big(\ \mu_\theta+\sigma_{t+1}^2\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(y\mid\mu_\theta),\ \ \sigma_{t+1}^2 I\ \Big)\;}$$

Same width \(\sigma_{t+1}^2\); the mean is pushed by \(\sigma_{t+1}^2\) along the classifier gradient — a nudge toward regions the classifier calls "\(y\)." This is exactly the Step‑2 identity, now realised inside the sampler.

Step 5 — where the guidance scale \(w\) comes from. The derivation above gives coefficient exactly 1 — honest Bayesian conditioning. In practice that pull is too weak, so you amplify the classifier term by a scale \(w\):

$$\text{mean}=\mu_\theta+\,w\,\sigma_{t+1}^2\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(y\mid\mu_\theta)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{replace } p_\phi(y\mid x_t)\ \text{by}\ p_\phi(y\mid x_t)^{\,w}\ \text{in Step 1}.$$

Raising the classifier to the power \(w\) sharpens it (mass piles onto the most confidently‑\(y\) regions), so \(w>1\) trades diversity for stronger class adherence; \(w{=}1\) is pure Bayes, \(w{=}0\) is unconditional. So \(w\) is a deliberate knob bolted on after the derivation — the math itself produced \(w{=}1\).

  • No retraining of the generator — pure sampling technique.
  • But the classifier must be trained (cross‑entropy) on noisy images \(x_t\) at every noise level — an off‑the‑shelf classifier won't do — plus an extra classifier forward+backward pass per step, and \(w\) needs tuning. Those three costs are exactly what CFG (6.2) removes.

6.2 Classifier‑free guidance (CFG) — the default everywhere

Refined goal: get the same effect with generation weights only (no classifier on noisy data, no extra model).

The implicit classifier — derived in three steps

Step 1 — a classifier is hiding inside the two generators (Bayes again). By Bayes, \(p(y\mid x_t)=\dfrac{p(x_t\mid y)\,p(y)}{p(x_t)}\). Take \(\log\) then \(\nabla_{x_t}\); the \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y)\) term is zero (no \(x_t\) in it):

$$\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y\mid x_t)=\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y)-\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t).$$

So the classifier gradient 6.1 needed is just conditional score − unconditional score — and you can get both scores from one network, no classifier trained at all.

Step 2 — substitute into classifier guidance. 6.1's guided (conditional) score, with scale \(w\), was \(\nabla\log p(x_t)+w\,\nabla\log p(y\mid x_t)\). Drop in Step 1:

$$s_{\text{guided}}=\nabla\log p(x_t)+w\big(\nabla\log p(x_t\mid y)-\nabla\log p(x_t)\big).$$

Step 3 — rewrite in \(\varepsilon\)-space. Predicted noise and score are one linear map apart, \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,\cdot)=-\sigma\,\nabla\log p(x_t,\cdot)\). Multiply the line above by \(-\sigma\), and name the two calls \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)=-\sigma\nabla\log p(x_t)\) (unconditional) and \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,y)=-\sigma\nabla\log p(x_t\mid y)\) (conditional):

$$\boxed{\;\varepsilon_{\text{CFG}}(x_t,y)=\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)+w\big(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,y)-\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)\big)\;}$$

One network, called twice — once with the condition \(y\), once with the null token \(\varnothing\) — reconstructs the classifier gradient with no classifier. That is the whole trick, and it is why CFG needs only generation weights.

Training: train a single \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t,y)\) but with condition dropout — replace \(y\) with \(\varnothing\) with probability \(p_{\text{uncond}}\) (best \(10\text{–}20\%\)). The same weights then give both scores.

The geometry (the one diagram to remember)

On a line: start at the unconditional prediction \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)\), step toward the conditional one \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,y)\), and extrapolate past it by factor \(w\). \(w{=}1\) lands exactly on the conditional sample; \(w{>}1\) overshoots — amplifying prompt adherence at the cost of diversity, and if pushed too far, pushing samples out of distribution (over‑saturation, artifacts). Typical \(w\approx3\text{–}8\) for images. Cost: still two forward passes per step.

CFG modifies the network's output before each reverse step, so it works unchanged with DDPM, DDIM, EDM, or flow — see §7.

7Guidance in score‑based & flow‑matching models

Because "predict noise", "predict score", and "predict velocity" are the same information (Fact 2), guidance transfers to all of them by editing whatever the network outputs.

7.1 Score‑based models — guidance is score addition

Here guidance is at its most literal. The guided score is just:

$$s_{\text{guided}}(x,y)=\nabla\log p(x)+w\,\big(\underbrace{\nabla\log p(x\mid y)-\nabla\log p(x)}_{\text{implicit classifier / classifier gradient}}\big).$$

Classifier guidance adds an explicit \(\nabla\log p(y\mid x)\); CFG builds the same term from conditional − unconditional scores. You then run the same Langevin/SDE/ODE sampler on \(s_{\text{guided}}\) instead of \(s\). Nothing else changes — guidance and the sampler are orthogonal.

7.2 Flow matching — guide the velocity

Train the velocity field with condition dropout exactly like CFG, then combine at sampling time:

$$u_{\text{CFG}}(x,t,y)=u_\theta(x,t,\varnothing)+w\big(u_\theta(x,t,y)-u_\theta(x,t,\varnothing)\big),\qquad\text{integrate } \dot x=u_{\text{CFG}}.$$

This is exactly how SD3 / Flux do text guidance. But why is guiding the velocity the same as guiding the score we derived in §6–7.1? It rests on one fact: velocity is an affine function of the score, with condition‑independent coefficients. Here it is, derived.

Why velocity‑CFG = score‑CFG — derived in two steps

Step 1 — the velocity↔score relation, derived slowly. Take the Gaussian path \(x_t=\alpha_t x_1+\sigma_t x_0\) with noise \(x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) and data \(x_1\) (rectified flow: \(\alpha_t=t,\ \sigma_t=1-t\)). The plan: write both the velocity and the score as conditional expectations of the two unknowns \(\mathbb E[x_1\mid x]\) and \(\mathbb E[x_0\mid x]\), then eliminate those two unknowns.

(a) Velocity = posterior mean of the conditional velocity. Differentiate the path in \(t\): \(\dot x_t=\dot\alpha_t x_1+\dot\sigma_t x_0\). By the §5.3 marginalization, the marginal field is that quantity's posterior mean:

$$u_t(x)=\mathbb E[\dot x_t\mid x_t{=}x]=\dot\alpha_t\,\mathbb E[x_1\mid x]+\dot\sigma_t\,\mathbb E[x_0\mid x].$$

(b) Score = −(expected noise)/\(\sigma_t\) (Tweedie). For any "signal + Gaussian noise" law \(x=\mu+\sigma z\), the score is \(\nabla\log p(x)=-\mathbb E[z\mid x]/\sigma\) — the same denoising identity used in §1/§3. Here \(z=x_0,\ \sigma=\sigma_t\):

$$s_t(x):=\nabla\log p_t(x)=-\tfrac{1}{\sigma_t}\,\mathbb E[x_0\mid x]\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \mathbb E[x_0\mid x]=-\sigma_t\,s_t(x).$$

(c) A consistency equation. Take \(\mathbb E[\,\cdot\mid x_t{=}x]\) of the path itself; since \(\mathbb E[x_t\mid x_t{=}x]=x\):

$$x=\alpha_t\,\mathbb E[x_1\mid x]+\sigma_t\,\mathbb E[x_0\mid x].$$

(d) Solve for the two expectations. Put (b)'s \(\mathbb E[x_0\mid x]=-\sigma_t s_t\) into (c):

$$x=\alpha_t\,\mathbb E[x_1\mid x]-\sigma_t^2 s_t\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \mathbb E[x_1\mid x]=\frac{x+\sigma_t^2 s_t}{\alpha_t}.$$

(e) Substitute both back into (a):

$$u_t(x)=\dot\alpha_t\cdot\frac{x+\sigma_t^2 s_t}{\alpha_t}+\dot\sigma_t(-\sigma_t s_t) =\underbrace{\tfrac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}}_{a_t}\,x+\underbrace{\Big(\tfrac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\sigma_t^2-\dot\sigma_t\sigma_t\Big)}_{b_t}\,s_t(x).$$

So \(u_t=a_t x+b_t s_t\) — affine in the score, with \(a_t,b_t\) depending only on the schedule \((\alpha_t,\sigma_t)\), not on the condition \(y\). Concretely for rectified flow (\(\alpha_t=t,\ \sigma_t=1-t\)): \(a_t=\tfrac1t,\ b_t=\tfrac{1-t}{t}\), i.e. \(u_t(x)=\tfrac1t\,x+\tfrac{1-t}{t}\,s_t(x)\) — one clean line turning the score into the velocity.

Step 2 — the affine map makes the two CFGs identical. Both calls share \(a_t,b_t\); only the score differs, so:

$$\begin{aligned} u_{\text{CFG}}&=u(x\mid\varnothing)+w\big(u(x\mid y)-u(x\mid\varnothing)\big)\\ &=\big[a_t x+b_t s(x\mid\varnothing)\big]+w\,b_t\big(s(x\mid y)-s(x\mid\varnothing)\big)\\ &=a_t x+b_t\underbrace{\big[s(x\mid\varnothing)+w(s(x\mid y)-s(x\mid\varnothing))\big]}_{=\,s_{\text{CFG}}}=a_t x+b_t\,s_{\text{CFG}}(x). \end{aligned}$$

So guiding the velocities gives exactly the affine map applied to the guided score. Why it goes through: the \(a_t x\) term is the same for conditional and unconditional, so it cancels in the difference \(u(y)-u(\varnothing)\); the \(b_t\) factor is the same for both, so it factors out. In words: CFG only uses "uncond \(+\,w(\)cond\(-\)uncond\()\)", and the score→velocity conversion is affine with condition‑independent coefficients, so the conversion commutes with that linear combination.

The "CFG behaves a little differently on velocities" caveat (5.7 issues) is practical, not structural: since \(s\) and \(u\) have different scales across \(t\), a fixed \(w\) can feel stronger/weaker at different noise levels, so \(w\) often needs retuning between the two parametrizations. The extrapolation form is identical, as shown.

So the extrapolation picture from §6.2 carries over exactly — you're pushing the transport direction further toward the conditioned target.

The unifying statement

Guidance = take the network's output, add \(w\times\)(conditional − unconditional), integrate as usual. Whether the output is \(\varepsilon\), a score, or a velocity, and whether the sampler is an SDE or an ODE, the recipe is identical. That's why you learn it once.

CFG is still the workhorse in every open text‑to‑image/video model — but "plain CFG at a fixed scale" is not the frontier anymore. What's actually used now:

The one problem they all attack — and how each does it

Every technique above is fixing the same flaw. CFG at high \(w\) buys prompt adherence by extrapolating away from the unconditional baseline, \(\varepsilon_\varnothing+w(\varepsilon_y-\varepsilon_\varnothing)\) — but that same over‑extrapolation does three ugly things: it inflates the prediction's magnitude (colors blow out / over‑saturate), it collapses diversity (shoves samples toward the data's average / mode), and it only helps at some noise levels. Each method targets one of those:

  • Guidance interval — targets "only helps at some noise levels." Apply \(w>1\) only in a middle band of noise; use \(w{=}1\) at the ends. Why: at very high noise everything is blurry, so strong guidance just amplifies coarse errors; at very low noise the image is nearly finished, so it over‑sharpens/saturates fine detail. The steering that matters is mid‑noise, where the content is decided. Gating \(w\) by \(t\) is a big FID win for almost no cost.
  • CFG rescaling / dynamic thresholding — targets magnitude inflation. After forming the guided prediction, rescale it so its per‑image std matches the unconditional (or clip extreme percentiles). Restores sane contrast so you can keep a high \(w\) without the "deep‑fried" look.
  • Autoguidance — targets diversity collapse, by fixing the baseline itself (details below).
  • Negative prompts — generalize the baseline: replace \(\varnothing\) with a chosen "bad" condition \(y^-\) (\(\varepsilon(y^-)+w(\varepsilon(y)-\varepsilon(y^-))\)), so you actively push away from named junk ("blurry, extra fingers").
  • Guidance distillation — targets the 2× cost: CFG needs a conditional and unconditional pass every step; train one network pass to reproduce the guided output, often fused with step‑distillation → 1–4‑step models (SDXL‑Turbo, LCM, Flux‑schnell).

Autoguidance (Karras 2024), in full — because it's the subtle one. Why CFG hurts diversity: the unconditional \(\varnothing\) differs from the conditional in two ways at once — it lacks the prompt and it models a broader distribution — so extrapolating away from it over‑corrects: you amplify prompt adherence but also drag samples toward the data mode, killing variety and saturating color. \(\varnothing\) is a bad "worse version of the task." The fix: guide with a deliberately weaker copy of the same conditional model — a smaller net, or an earlier/under‑trained checkpoint, with the same prompt: \(u_{\text{good}}+w\,(u_{\text{good}}-u_{\text{bad}})\). Because the bad model makes the same kinds of mistakes as the good one, only more, the difference \((u_{\text{good}}-u_{\text{bad}})\) isolates the pure competence direction — the errors your model already half‑fixed — with no distribution shift. Extrapolating along it raises quality without the diversity/saturation tax. When it beats CFG / use cases: when the target is raw sample quality / FID — benchmarks, research, high‑end generation — because it decouples "make it better" from "make it more on‑prompt," which CFG fatally conflates (it set SOTA FID on ImageNet). Cost: you need a "bad twin," but an early training checkpoint or a small model suffices — cheap. It's not yet the default in shipped text‑to‑image (those still lean on CFG + interval/rescale, or distillation), largely because of that extra‑model bookkeeping.

The density each guidance rule actually samples — one lemma, two instances

Every rule here has the shape anchor + weight × (target − anchor). One derivation covers them all and shows exactly why CFG taxes diversity and autoguidance doesn't. Write \(D\) for the network output (an \(\varepsilon\), a velocity \(u\), or a denoiser): each is \(\propto-\sigma_t\nabla\!\log p\) of whatever distribution that branch learned, so a linear combination of outputs is the same linear combination of scores — the \(-\sigma_t\) multiplies every term and cancels.

$$\tilde D=D_A+w\,(D_B-D_A)\ \Longrightarrow\ \nabla\!\log\tilde p=\nabla\!\log p_A+w\,\nabla\!\log\frac{p_B}{p_A}\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{\ \tilde p\ \propto\ p_A\!\left(\frac{p_B}{p_A}\right)^{\!w}=\,p_A^{\,1-w}\,p_B^{\,w}\ }$$

(The middle step integrates the gradient back up in \(x\); \(\nabla\log p(c)\)‑type constants vanish because they don't depend on \(x\).) Now plug in the two anchors:

  • CFG — anchor \(A=\varnothing\) (unconditional), target \(B=y\) (conditional). The ratio is the classifier posterior:
    $$\frac{p_B}{p_A}=\frac{p(x\mid c)}{p(x)}=\frac{p(c\mid x)}{p(c)}\propto p(c\mid x)\quad\Rightarrow\quad \tilde p\propto p(x)\,\big[p(c\mid x)\big]^{w}.$$
    Neutral at \(w{=}1\) (pure conditional). The exponent lands on \(p(c\mid x)\) — raising a posterior to a power \(>1\) shrinks its entropy and piles mass onto the \(x\) the classifier is most sure is class \(c\): the class‑prototypical, exaggerated, over‑saturated examples. That's the diversity/saturation tax, read straight off the formula.
  • Autoguidance — anchor \(A=\text{bad}\), target \(B=\text{good}\), both conditioned on the same \(c\):
    $$\tilde p\propto p_{\text{bad}}\!\left(\frac{p_{\text{good}}}{p_{\text{bad}}}\right)^{\!w}=\,p_{\text{good}}\!\left(\frac{p_{\text{good}}}{p_{\text{bad}}}\right)^{\!w-1}.$$
    Neutral at \(w{=}1\) (pure good model). Same operation — temper a ratio by an exponent — but the base is now a quality ratio of two same‑class densities. It spikes where the good model has structure the bad one missed (fine detail, sharp edges, correct anatomy), not at the class centroid; and because both are trained on the same conditional data their supports match, so tempering sharpens fidelity without shoving samples toward a narrower distribution. No diversity tax. (Some papers anchor on the good model, \(u_{\text{good}}+w'(u_{\text{good}}-u_{\text{bad}})\) — identical field with \(w'=w-1\).)

The whole story in one line: both rules do the same thing — exponentiate a ratio — and differ only in the base. CFG's base is \(p(c\mid x)\), which peaks at the class mode → kills variety; autoguidance's base is \(p_{\text{good}}/p_{\text{bad}}\), which peaks at "fine detail the weak model missed" → raises quality only. So yes, there is a "\(w\) power" in autoguidance too — it's just applied to a benign quantity.

The "bad twin" — what Karras 2024 actually degraded, and the one trap

What they used (not every knob at once). The bad model \(D_{\text{bad}}\) was the same model degraded along a small, compatible set of axes — chiefly less capacity (a smaller net) and less training (fewer iterations, or an earlier checkpoint of the same run); they also tested adding input noise / dropout. Same data, same architecture family, same conditioning \(c\). The record‑setting ImageNet FID used a twin that was both smaller and trained less. The rule they landed on: the degradations must be compatible — they make the twin worse at the same task, not broken in a different way.

Why "compatible" is the whole game (the part that wasn't clear). The guidance direction is \(D_{\text{good}}-D_{\text{bad}}\). That difference is a clean "make it better" arrow only if the two models are doing the identical job and one is simply weaker — then subtracting cancels everything they agree on and leaves exactly the competence the good model added. If instead the twin differs in kind — different architecture, different conditioning, different training data — the subtraction no longer cancels cleanly: \(D_{\text{good}}-D_{\text{bad}}\) mixes "more competent" with "different task," and extrapolating along it drags samples toward whatever the twin uniquely lacks (a garbage direction) → artifacts. So the rule is: degrade only along axes your good model is already limited by; never add a new, orthogonal weakness. An earlier EMA snapshot of the same run is the safest and cheapest twin — by construction it's the same model, just less trained, so \(D_{\text{good}}-D_{\text{bad}}\) is pure competence.

CFG++ — and why swapping in a different \(\varepsilon\) doesn't "break the math"

First, a correction to the table above's old label: CFG++ is not the same as CFG‑rescaling/dynamic‑thresholding (they're often lumped together). Rescaling post‑scales the guided output's magnitude; CFG++ changes the sampler update itself. Recall a DDIM step has three pieces:

$$\underbrace{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}_{\text{(1) predict noise}}\qquad \underbrace{\hat x_0=\frac{x_t-\sigma_t\varepsilon}{\alpha_t}}_{\text{(2) clean anchor}}\qquad \underbrace{x_{t-1}=\alpha_{t-1}\hat x_0+\sigma_{t-1}\varepsilon}_{\text{(3) re‑noise to next level}}$$

What breaks in plain CFG. It plugs the extrapolated \(\tilde\varepsilon=\varepsilon_\varnothing+w(\varepsilon_y-\varepsilon_\varnothing)\) into both (2) and (3). At high \(w\) that \(\tilde\varepsilon\) is an over‑shoot, so the clean anchor \(\hat x_0\) becomes an over‑sharpened, over‑saturated image that no real photo looks like — it's off the data manifold. Every step re‑noises from that bad anchor, compounding the error; and because the field isn't invertible, DDIM inversion (running the sampler backward to recover the noise latent of a real image so you can edit it) drifts.

What CFG++ does. Use the guided \(\tilde\varepsilon\) only to decide where / how far to step, but take the clean anchor \(\hat x_0\) in (2) from the (near‑)conditional denoiser — a genuine denoiser output, hence an on‑manifold, plausible image — with a bounded knob \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) instead of an unbounded \(w\). Every intermediate \(\hat x_0\) stays a real‑looking image, so trajectories don't deep‑fry and inversion stays stable.

Now your actual question — doesn't using a "wrong" \(\varepsilon\) break the math? The honest answer: all guidance is already a heuristic. The guided field \(\tilde\varepsilon(x,t)\) is not the exact score of any single normalized distribution consistent across all noise levels — CFG only approximately samples \(p(x)p(c\mid x)^w\). So there is no "right" \(\varepsilon\) once you decide to guide; the sampler just integrates whatever vector field you hand it, and the "right" \(\varepsilon_\theta(x,c)\) is only right for the unguided conditional. Given that, CFG++ is arguably more correct, not less: because the conditional denoiser produces a valid clean image, re‑noising from it never leaves the manifold, whereas the extrapolated anchor does. You're not corrupting a sound derivation — you're choosing the tilt whose every intermediate estimate is still a plausible image.

Jargon used above: T2I = text‑to‑image (SD/SDXL, FLUX, Imagen…). EDM2 = Karras 2024's class‑conditional ImageNet diffusion line — the "research models where you own the whole training stack," where autoguidance was demonstrated. Inversion / editing = take a real photo, run the sampler backward to its noise latent, change the prompt, re‑denoise → an edited version; this needs an invertible, on‑manifold sampler, which is exactly where CFG++ (and modest \(w\)) matter.

What to actually do — a decision list
  1. Default — plain CFG. Train with condition dropout \(p_{\text{uncond}}\approx0.1\text{–}0.2\) (so one net learns both the conditional and unconditional branch), sample with scale \(w\approx3\text{–}8\) for images (the raw conditional is too weak; \(w{>}1\) sharpens adherence). This alone gets ~95% of the way.
  2. Over‑saturated at high \(w\)? Fix the symptom before lowering \(w\). Add CFG rescaling and/or a guidance interval first — they remove the saturation/artifacts while keeping prompt adherence, whereas dropping \(w\) throws the adherence away.
  3. Chasing FID / max quality? Use autoguidance. It beats CFG precisely on the quality‑vs‑diversity frontier (no diversity collapse), at the cost of maintaining a weak "bad twin" of your model. This is the "paper‑number" / high‑end‑quality choice, not the default production one.
  4. Need it fast? Distill the guidance. Fold the two‑pass guided output into a single network pass (often with step‑distillation), so you're back to one forward per step and 1–4 steps total.
  5. Classifier guidance? Basically retired for text/image — the "train a classifier on noisy images at every level" requirement killed it. It survives only where you genuinely have a differentiable reward or classifier you want to steer by (some scientific / controllable‑generation / reward‑tilting settings).

9Where it lives: U‑Net vs ViT/DiT

Two things to inject: the condition signal, and how the backbone consumes it.

Those three "how it's injected" cells are three mechanisms — and they're usually stated as jargon. Here is each one end‑to‑end, no hand‑waving; 9.1–9.2 then just say where they sit in a U‑Net vs a DiT.

Mechanism 1 — Modulation (adaLN‑Zero / adaGN), step by step

What it's for: inject a single global vector (timestep, class, pooled prompt) so it tilts the whole network — without adding any tokens.

  1. Build one condition vector \(c\). Embed the timestep (sinusoidal → MLP); embed the class / pooled text; sum them into \(c\in\mathbb R^d\).
  2. Per block, map \(c\) to modulation parameters. A small MLP: \(c\mapsto(\gamma,\beta,\alpha)\) for each normalized sub‑layer. (adaLN‑Zero emits six vectors per block — scale, shift, gate for the attention path, and again for the MLP path.)
  3. Normalize, then re‑inject scale & shift. LayerNorm first strips each token's own mean/variance; modulation writes the condition's back in:
    $$h_{\text{mod}}=(1+\gamma)\odot\text{LayerNorm}(h)+\beta.$$
    \(\gamma\) sets each channel's gain, \(\beta\) its offset — both dictated by \(c\).
  4. Run the sub‑layer, then gate the residual. \(h\leftarrow h+\alpha\odot\text{SubLayer}(h_{\text{mod}})\), where SubLayer is the block's attention or MLP and \(\alpha\) is a per‑channel gate on its update.
  5. Zero‑init the gate. \(\alpha=0\) at start ⇒ \(h\leftarrow h\) (identity), so the network ignores the condition at init and opens the gates as it trains — the "‑Zero" that makes deep DiTs train stably.

How it works / impact: cheap (a few vectors per block, no new tokens) and it touches every block, so a global signal pervades the whole net; the zero‑init removes the early instability random modulation would cause. Intuition: normalization erases a feature's scale/offset, and the condition writes back "how loud, and where‑centered, each channel should be at this timestep." (A U‑Net does the identical thing to its GroupNorm — that's "adaGN.")

Mechanism 2 — Cross‑attention, step by step

What it's for: inject a variable‑length sequence (per‑token text from T5/CLIP, or another image's features) so each image location can pull exactly the piece it needs.

  1. Two token sets. Flatten the \(H\times W\) feature map → \(N\) image tokens; take the \(M\) condition tokens (e.g. words).
  2. Project — this is what makes it "cross." Queries come from the image, keys and values from the condition:
    $$Q=X_{\text{img}}W_Q,\qquad K=X_{\text{cond}}W_K,\qquad V=X_{\text{cond}}W_V.$$
  3. Relevance scores. For image token \(i\) and condition token \(j\): \(s_{ij}=Q_i\!\cdot\!K_j/\sqrt d\) — "how relevant is word \(j\) to location \(i\)."
  4. Softmax over the condition. \(a_{ij}=\text{softmax}_j(s_{ij})\): each image location gets a distribution over the words.
  5. Blend the values. \(\text{out}_i=\sum_j a_{ij}\,V_j\) — location \(i\) pulls in a weighted mix of exactly the words it attended to.
  6. Residual add, in every block. \(X_{\text{img}}\leftarrow X_{\text{img}}+\text{out}\).

How it works / impact: handles any condition length (one word or 300) and is spatially selective — the region destined to become "a dog" attends to the token "dog." That per‑location selectivity is why text uses attention, not modulation. Cost: an \(N\times M\) attention per layer. MM‑DiT variant (SD3 / Flux): concatenate image + text into one sequence and run self‑attention over the union, so text also reads the image (two‑way), instead of image‑queries‑text (one‑way).

Mechanism 3 — ControlNet, step by step (and how it fits every method)

What it's for: add a pixel‑aligned map (depth, Canny edges, pose, segmentation, a reference image) to an already‑trained model — precise spatial control, without retraining the base or damaging it.

  1. Freeze the pretrained denoiser (U‑Net or DiT). Its weights never change, so the base capability is protected.
  2. Clone its encoder. Make a trainable copy of the down‑blocks; this parallel branch will read the control.
  3. Feed the control in. Encode the control image with a small conv stem and add it at the copy's input.
  4. Run the copy → it produces features at each resolution, the same shapes as the frozen encoder's.
  5. Inject through zero‑convs. At each injection point, add \(\text{zeroconv}(\text{copy feature})\) into the frozen network's matching feature. The \(1\times1\) "zero convolution" is initialized to 0.
  6. Zero‑init ⇒ safe start. At step 0 the zero‑convs output nothing, so ControlNet adds zero and the frozen model behaves exactly as before — no shock, no forgetting. Training gradually opens the zero‑convs, letting control features seep in.
  7. Train only the copy + zero‑convs on (control, image) pairs, using the same loss the base was trained with.

How it fits the methods (your question): ControlNet is method‑agnostic. It never touches the objective or the sampler — it only edits the denoiser's internal features, which still predict \(\varepsilon\) / \(x_0\) / velocity as before. So the identical recipe (freeze base → add trainable encoder copy → zero‑convs → train with the base's own loss) drops onto a DDPM U‑Net, an EDM model, or a flow‑matching DiT alike, and CFG still stacks on top. It's a conditioning attachment, orthogonal to the noise/velocity math — which is exactly why one idea serves all of §1–§5.

Impact: reuse a huge pretrained model, gain exact spatial control by training only a fraction of the parameters, suffer no catastrophic forgetting (zero‑init), and hot‑swap control types (a depth ControlNet, a pose ControlNet, …) on the same frozen base.

9.1 In a U‑Net (SD 1.x/2; this repo's unet.py)

  • Timestep & global condition: sinusoidal \(t\) embedding → MLP → added as a per‑channel bias in every residual block. Already in the repo (time_proj); a class/text vector just sums into that same embedding. Scaling/shifting the GroupNorm with it = "adaGN".
  • Text prompt: add a cross‑attention layer in each block — flattened \(H\times W\) feature map queries the text tokens. The repo's U‑Net has no attention yet — this is the piece you'd add for text.
  • Spatial (ControlNet): a trainable encoder copy injects control features via zero‑init convolutions.

9.2 In a DiT (transformer backbone — SD3, Flux, MIRA; now in this repo)

Patchify the (latent) image into tokens, run transformer blocks, unpatchify. Conditioning changes shape:

  • Global condition — adaLN‑Zero (the DiT signature): map \((t,y_{\text{global}})\) through an MLP to produce, per block, six vectors — scale & shift for two LayerNorms plus two residual gates \(\alpha\):
    $$\text{modulate}(h)=(1+\gamma)\,\text{LayerNorm}(h)+\beta,\qquad h\leftarrow h+\alpha\cdot\text{Attn/MLP}(\cdot).$$
    The gates \(\alpha\) init to zero so each block starts as identity — that "adaLN‑Zero" init is why DiT trains stably. It's the transformer analogue of the U‑Net's adaGN.
  • Text / sequence condition: either cross‑attention (image tokens attend to text tokens), or joint / MM‑DiT attention (SD3, Flux): concatenate image + text tokens into one sequence and run self‑attention over the union, so both modalities read each other.
  • Positional info: transformers have no built‑in geometry, so add positional embeddings (learned / sincos / RoPE) to the patch tokens.
In this repo (now implemented)

models/dit.py — patchify conv, learned positional embedding, adaLN‑Zero blocks with fused self‑attention, zero‑init final layer. The clean mental model

Global scalars (timestep, class, pooled prompt) → modulate the normalization (adaGN in U‑Net, adaLN‑Zero in DiT). Variable‑length sequences (text, another image) → attention (cross‑attention, or joint self‑attention in MM‑DiT). Pixel‑aligned maps → concatenate / parallel branch. Guidance strength (CFG) is orthogonal — it edits the output, so it's identical across backbones.

12Distillation — turning 20–1000 steps into 1–4

Every method above pays the same tax at inference: integrating the reverse ODE/SDE costs one network evaluation (NFE) per step — ~1000 for ancestral DDPM, ~20–50 for DDIM/EDM, ~10–30 for flow matching. Distillation trains a student to reproduce the teacher's final image in 1–4 steps by learning to jump along the trajectory instead of crawling it. This is how SDXL‑Turbo, LCM, and Flux‑schnell run in real time.

The shared setup

Start from a trained teacher (any diffusion or FM model) and its deterministic sampler (DDIM / PF‑ODE / FM‑ODE). That sampler defines, for each noise \(x_T\), a fixed trajectory ending at a clean sample \(x_0\). Every distillation method is a different way to compress that teacher trajectory — either by taking bigger jumps along it (methods 1–2) or by matching the teacher's output distribution directly (method 3).

Method 1 — Progressive Distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022)

Idea: teach a student to do two teacher steps in one, then repeat — halving the step count each round: \(1024\to512\to\cdots\to4\to2\to1\).

  • Target: run the teacher's deterministic sampler for two steps, \(x_t\to x_{t-1}\to x_{t-2}\). The student \(f_\phi\) is trained so that one of its steps from \(x_t\) lands directly on the teacher's two‑step result \(x_{t-2}\):
    $$\mathcal L=\big\lVert f_\phi(x_t,t)\ -\ \underbrace{\text{DDIM}_{\text{teacher}}^{2\text{ steps}}(x_t)}_{\text{fixed target }x_{t-2}}\big\rVert^2.$$
  • Loop: distil student → it becomes the new teacher → distil again (halve steps) → … Each round the student needs half the NFEs of the last.
  • Inference: after \(k\) rounds, sample in \(N/2^k\) steps (down to 1–2). Deterministic.
  • Trade‑off: simple, stable, no adversary — but many training rounds, and quality softens toward 1 step.

Method 2 — Consistency Models / LCM (Song 2023; Luo 2023)

Idea: learn a function \(f_\phi(x_t,t)\) that maps any point on a trajectory straight to its endpoint (the clean sample) in one shot. If every point on a trajectory maps to the same endpoint, you can jump from pure noise to data in a single evaluation.

  • The self‑consistency constraint: two adjacent points on the same teacher trajectory must map to the same place:
    $$f_\phi(x_t,t)\ \approx\ f_\phi(x_{t'},t')\quad\text{for }t'
  • Training (consistency distillation): take a data point, noise it to \(x_t\); use one teacher ODE step to get the adjacent \(x_{t'}\); push the student's outputs at \(x_t\) and \(x_{t'}\) together (the target side uses an EMA "target network" for stability):
    $$\mathcal L=\big\lVert f_\phi(x_t,t)-f_{\phi^-}(x_{t'},t')\big\rVert^2,\qquad \phi^-=\text{EMA}(\phi).$$
  • Inference: 1 step \(x_0=f_\phi(\text{noise},T)\), or 2–4 steps of "\(f\) then re‑noise a little" for higher quality (the standard LCM multi‑step sampler).
  • LCM = consistency distillation done in a latent diffusion model (Stable Diffusion) — the version you actually see in ComfyUI/diffusers as "LCM‑LoRA," giving 4‑step SD. LCM‑LoRA distils only a small LoRA adapter, so it's cheap and swappable onto any SD checkpoint.
  • Trade‑off: genuinely 1–4 step; but pure 1‑step can look slightly soft/less diverse. No adversary needed.

Method 3 — Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD / DMD2, Yin 2023–24)

Idea: don't match the teacher's trajectory point‑by‑point — match its output distribution. Train a one‑step generator \(G_\phi(\text{noise})\to\) image so that the distribution of its samples equals the teacher's data distribution. This gives the best few‑step quality today (SDXL‑Turbo‑class), because it optimizes the thing you actually care about (the sample distribution), not path fidelity.

  • How you match distributions without densities: minimize an (approximate reverse) KL between the generator's output distribution \(p_G\) and the data distribution \(p_{\text{data}}\). Its gradient needs only the difference of two scores:
    $$\nabla_\phi \mathrm{KL}(p_G\,\|\,p_{\text{data}})\ \propto\ \mathbb E\big[\ \underbrace{s_{\text{fake}}(x)}_{\text{score of }p_G}-\underbrace{s_{\text{real}}(x)}_{\text{teacher's score}}\ \big].$$
    Real score = the frozen teacher (it already learned \(\nabla\log p_{\text{data}}\)). Fake score = a second diffusion model trained online to score the generator's own current outputs. The generator is nudged so its samples look, to the teacher's score, like real data.
  • Training loop (three players, GAN‑like but score‑based): (1) generator makes a one‑step image from noise; (2) update the fake‑score net on the generator's outputs (a normal diffusion loss); (3) update the generator using \(s_{\text{fake}}-s_{\text{real}}\). DMD2 adds a GAN discriminator and drops an expensive regression term for stability/quality.
  • Inference: 1 step (or 4 for DMD2's best numbers). Deterministic generator.
  • Trade‑off: SOTA few‑step fidelity and diversity; but the training is heavier (two/three networks, adversarial) and fiddlier than consistency.

Two different things get distilled — guidance vs steps

A "fast guided model" compresses two independent costs; it helps to see them apart.

Guidance distillation vs step distillation — one stage or two?
  • Step distillation (methods 1–3 above) attacks the many‑steps cost: 20–1000 → 1–4 steps.
  • Guidance distillation attacks the other cost — CFG needs two network passes per step (conditional + unconditional). Train the student to reproduce the guided output in a single pass, usually taking the guidance scale \(w\) as an extra input embedding so it stays tunable. (This is exactly why FLUX & friends take a guidance scalar as input — the 2× is already folded in.) On its own it's still multi‑step.

One stage or two? Historically two separate stages (Salimans & Ho: guidance‑distil, then progressively step‑distil). Modern practice usually fuses them: the teacher you sample targets from is already the guided sampler, so the student learns guidance and step‑reduction in one distillation. Both are valid; fused is now the norm (LCM/Turbo distil from a CFG teacher, so the few‑step student bakes guidance in).

Fused algorithm, concretely: (1) pick scale(s) \(w\); (2) teacher target = run the CFG‑guided deterministic sampler (2 passes/step) for the needed steps; (3) train the student to match that guided trajectory/endpoint in one pass and few steps via any of methods 1–3; (4) optionally condition the student on \(w\) so it's adjustable at inference. Out comes a model that is one pass/step, 1–4 steps, guidance already inside.

Can you distil autoguidance down to a single model? Yes.

Autoguidance costs two things: (a) at training time you must build/keep a bad twin; (b) at inference it runs two passes per step (good + bad) — the same 2× as CFG. Your instinct is right: the inference 2× is removable exactly like guidance distillation — set the teacher to be the autoguided sampler (good + bad) and train a student to reproduce its output in one pass. Nothing about the recipe is special; it's the same as distilling CFG, so "needs a second model at inference" is not inherent.

What remains is only the training‑time need for a bad twin to create that teacher — and an early EMA snapshot makes that nearly free. So a single‑model, autoguidance‑distilled student is entirely possible; it simply hasn't propagated through shipped stacks yet (autoguidance is a 2024 result; production distillation pipelines lag). The barrier is ecosystem maturity, not the math.

How to run (practical)

Off‑the‑shelf (no training): in 🤗 diffusers, load an LCMScheduler and fuse an lcm-lora adapter onto any SD/SDXL checkpoint → generate at num_inference_steps=4, guidance_scale≈1.
