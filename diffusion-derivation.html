Diffusion models, derived in full — from the ELBO to $\eps$ to DDIM

A rigorous, self-contained walk through the mathematics of denoising diffusion, at the level of Ho et al. (2020) and Song et al. (2021). Every identity is derived — the ELBO, its exact decomposition into KL terms, the tractable posterior by completing the square, the collapse of the loss to noise-prediction MSE, and the non-Markovian construction behind DDIM. Nothing is asserted without proof; nothing is skipped.

0 Ground rules

Setup and notation

We model a data distribution $q(\x_0)$ over $\x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (e.g. an image). A diffusion model introduces a sequence of latent variables $\x_1,\dots,\x_T$ of the same dimension, tied together by two Markov chains running in opposite directions. Fix this vocabulary and the whole derivation stays legible.

$\x_0 \sim q(\x_0)$a clean data point (the thing we want to generate)
$\x_1,\dots,\x_T$progressively noisier latents; $\x_T$ is (almost) pure noise
$q$the forward (noising) process — fixed by us, no parameters
$p_\theta$the reverse (denoising) process — a neural network with parameters $\theta$
$\beta_1,\dots,\beta_T \in (0,1)$the variance schedule (a design choice)
$\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t,\quad \ab_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s$shorthand that makes the marginals clean
$\N(\x;\mu,\Sigma)$a Gaussian density in $\x$ with mean $\mu$, covariance $\Sigma$
$\KL(q\,\|\,p)$Kullback–Leibler divergence, $\E_{q}[\log(q/p)] \ge 0$

Our single goal is maximum likelihood: make the observed data probable under the model, i.e. maximize $\log p_\theta(\x_0)$. Every equation below is machinery for doing that when the likelihood itself is an intractable integral.

1 The objects

Two Markov chains, pointed opposite ways

The forward process $q$ — fixed

Start from data and add a little Gaussian noise at each step, with variance set by the schedule $\beta_t$:

\[ \fwd{q(\x_t \mid \x_{t-1})} = \N\!\big(\x_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\x_{t-1},\ \beta_t \Id\big),\qquad q(\x_{1:T}\mid \x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(\x_t\mid \x_{t-1}). \]

Two facts we will lean on repeatedly. First, it is Markov: $\x_t$ depends only on $\x_{t-1}$. Second, it has no learnable parameters — we choose $\beta_t$ once and never touch it again. The scaling factor $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ (slightly less than 1) shrinks the signal a touch at each step while $\beta_t\Id$ injects noise; over many steps the data is annihilated.

The reverse process $p_\theta$ — learned

The generative model runs the chain backward, starting from pure noise and denoising one step at a time:

\[ p_\theta(\x_{0:T}) = p(\x_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T} \rev{p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid \x_t)},\qquad \rev{p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid \x_t)} = \N\!\big(\x_{t-1};\ \mu_\theta(\x_t,t),\ \Sigma_\theta(\x_t,t)\big), \]

with the fixed prior $p(\x_T)=\N(\x_T;\mathbf{0},\Id)$. The only parameters in the entire model live in $\mu_\theta$ (and possibly $\Sigma_\theta$). We assume each reverse step is Gaussian; §8 proves when that assumption is justified. To generate a sample: draw $\x_T\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$ and iterate $\x_T \to \x_{T-1}\to\dots\to\x_0$.

Sampling $p_\theta(\x_0)=\int p_\theta(\x_{0:T})\,d\x_{1:T}$ is an integral over every possible noising trajectory — intractable. That intractable integral is exactly what the ELBO exists to route around (§4).

2 The one-shot jump

The forward marginal $q(\x_t\mid\x_0)$, derived by induction

A remarkable property: although $q$ is defined step by step, we can jump from $\x_0$ to any $\x_t$ in closed form. This is what makes training cheap, and it drives every later simplification. Using the reparameterization $\x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\x_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\eps_{t-1}$ with $\eps_{t-1}\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$ (which is just $q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1})$ written as a sample), unroll the recursion:

  1. \[ \x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\eps_{t-1} \]

    One forward step, sampled. $\eps_{t-1}\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$.

  2. \[ = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\big(\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}}\,\x_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}}\,\eps_{t-2}\big) + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\eps_{t-1} \]

    Substitute the previous step. Distribute the $\sqrt{\alpha_t}$.

  3. \[ = \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\x_{t-2} + \underbrace{\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})}\,\eps_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\eps_{t-1}}_{\text{two independent Gaussians}} \]

    The two noise terms are independent, zero-mean Gaussians with variances $\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})$ and $(1-\alpha_t)$.

  4. \[ \sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})}\,\eps_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\eps_{t-1} \;=\; \sqrt{\,\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})+(1-\alpha_t)\,}\;\bar\eps \;=\; \sqrt{1-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\;\bar\eps \]

    Key merge: the sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, with variances adding. The two variances collapse: $\alpha_t-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}+1-\alpha_t = 1-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}$. Here $\bar\eps\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$.

  5. \[ \therefore\quad \x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\x_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\bar\eps \;\;\xrightarrow{\text{induct to }\x_0}\;\; \x_t = \sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0 + \sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps \]

    The recursion has the same shape one level down, so induction carries it all the way to $\x_0$: every intermediate noise merges into one $\eps\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$, and the products of $\alpha_s$ become $\ab_t=\prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$.

\[ \fwd{q(\x_t\mid\x_0)} = \N\!\big(\x_t;\ \sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0,\ (1-\ab_t)\Id\big) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \x_t = \sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0 + \sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps,\ \ \eps\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id). \]forward marginal

Read it off directly: the signal is scaled by $\sqrt{\ab_t}$ and buried under noise of scale $\sqrt{1-\ab_t}$. Since every $\alpha_s\lt 1$, the product $\ab_t\to 0$ as $t\to T$, so $\x_T$ becomes standard noise — which is exactly why sampling can start from $\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$.

4 The bound

The likelihood objective and the ELBO

We cannot evaluate $\log p_\theta(\x_0)$ directly, so we bound it. Multiply and divide inside the integral by the forward density $q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)$ — legal since it is strictly positive — turning the integral into an expectation, then apply Jensen's inequality ($\log$ is concave, so $\log\E[\,\cdot\,]\ge\E[\log\,\cdot\,]$):

  1. \[ \log p_\theta(\x_0) = \log\!\int p_\theta(\x_{0:T})\,d\x_{1:T} \]

    The marginal is the joint integrated over every latent — the intractable object.

  2. \[ = \log\!\int q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)\,\frac{p_\theta(\x_{0:T})}{q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)}\,d\x_{1:T} = \log\,\E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(\x_{0:T})}{q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)}\right] \]

    Importance-sampling trick: introduce $q$. Now it is an expectation under the forward chain — which we can sample from.

  3. \[ \ge\ \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(\x_{0:T})}{q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)}\right] \;=:\; L \qquad\text{(the ELBO)} \]

    Jensen. $L$ is a lower bound on the log-likelihood. We minimize $-L$, a valid upper bound on the negative log-likelihood.

The inequality's gap is not a mystery — it is exactly a KL divergence. Writing the reverse-time posterior $p_\theta(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)=p_\theta(\x_{0:T})/p_\theta(\x_0)$:

  1. \[ \KL\!\big(q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)\,\big\|\,p_\theta(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)\big) = \E_q\!\left[\log\frac{q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)}{p_\theta(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)}\right] = \E_q\!\left[\log\frac{q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)\,p_\theta(\x_0)}{p_\theta(\x_{0:T})}\right] \]

    Substitute $p_\theta(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)=p_\theta(\x_{0:T})/p_\theta(\x_0)$.

  2. \[ = -L + \log p_\theta(\x_0) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log p_\theta(\x_0) = L + \underbrace{\KL\!\big(q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)\,\big\|\,p_\theta(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)\big)}_{\ge\,0} \]

    $\log p_\theta(\x_0)$ pulls out of the expectation (constant in $\x_{1:T}$). Because KL $\ge 0$, this re-proves $L\le\log p_\theta(\x_0)$ exactly, and shows the gap is the mismatch between the forward chain and the model's true posterior.

i What we optimize

Maximizing $L$ does two things at once: it pushes up the likelihood and drives the model's posterior toward the fixed forward chain. Everything from here is algebra to turn $-L$ into something we can actually compute and differentiate.

5 The heart of it

Decomposing $-L$ into per-step KL divergences

As one expectation, $-L$ is opaque. The famous rewrite regroups it into a sum of terms, each a KL between two Gaussians we can write in closed form. The whole trick is to re-express each forward step by conditioning on $\x_0$ and applying Bayes' rule. Here is every step.

  1. \[ -L = \E_q\!\left[-\log\frac{p_\theta(\x_{0:T})}{q(\x_{1:T}\mid\x_0)}\right] = \E_q\!\left[-\log p(\x_T) - \sum_{t\ge 1}\log\frac{p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)}{q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1})}\right] \]

    Substitute the two factorized products from §1 and split the log of the ratio.

  2. \[ = \E_q\!\left[-\log p(\x_T) - \sum_{t\ge 2}\log\frac{p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)}{q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1})} - \log\frac{p_\theta(\x_0\mid\x_1)}{q(\x_1\mid\x_0)}\right] \]

    Peel off the $t=1$ term; we treat it separately at the end.

  3. \[ q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1}) \;\overset{\text{Markov}}{=}\; q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1},\x_0) \;\overset{\text{Bayes}}{=}\; \frac{q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)\,q(\x_t\mid\x_0)}{q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_0)} \]

    The pivot. Conditioning on $\x_0$ is free (the chain is Markov), and Bayes' rule flips the direction — introducing the tractable posterior $q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)$ we compute in §7.

  4. \[ \sum_{t\ge 2}\log\frac{p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)}{q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1})} = \sum_{t\ge 2}\log\frac{p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)}{q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)} + \sum_{t\ge 2}\log\frac{q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_0)}{q(\x_t\mid\x_0)} \]

    Substitute step 3 and split the log again. The second sum is a telescope.

  5. \[ \sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_0)}{q(\x_t\mid\x_0)} = \log q(\x_1\mid\x_0) - \log q(\x_T\mid\x_0) \]

    Telescoping cancellation: consecutive numerator/denominator terms annihilate; only the two endpoints survive. This is what conjures the prior term $L_T$ out of thin air.

  6. \[ -\log\frac{p_\theta(\x_0\mid\x_1)}{q(\x_1\mid\x_0)} = -\log p_\theta(\x_0\mid\x_1) + \log q(\x_1\mid\x_0) \]

    Expand the leftover $t=1$ term. Its $+\log q(\x_1\mid\x_0)$ will cancel the $-\log q(\x_1\mid\x_0)$ that step 5 contributes with the overall minus sign.

  7. \[ -L = \E_q\!\left[\ \log\frac{q(\x_T\mid\x_0)}{p(\x_T)} \;+\; \sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)}{p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)} \;-\; \log p_\theta(\x_0\mid\x_1)\ \right] \]

    Collect everything. The $\log q(\x_1\mid\x_0)$ pieces cancel exactly; $-\log p(\x_T)+\log q(\x_T\mid\x_0)$ becomes the first ratio; the denominators flip to put $q$ on top.

  8. \[ \E_q\!\left[\log\frac{q(\x_T\mid\x_0)}{p(\x_T)}\right] = \E_{\x_0}\,\KL\!\big(q(\x_T\mid\x_0)\,\|\,p(\x_T)\big) \]

    Recognize the KL. The integrand depends only on $\x_T$; marginalizing the rest of $q$ leaves $\x_T\sim q(\x_T\mid\x_0)$, and $\E_{q(\x_T\mid\x_0)}[\log(q/p)]$ is a KL by definition. Likewise each middle term becomes $\E_{q(\x_t,\x_0)}\KL(q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t))$.

\[ -L = \E_q\Big[\ \underbrace{\fwd{\KL\!\big(q(\x_T\mid\x_0)\,\|\,p(\x_T)\big)}}_{L_T}\ +\ \sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\rev{\KL\!\big(q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)\big)}}_{L_{t-1}}\ \underbrace{-\ \log p_\theta(\x_0\mid\x_1)}_{L_0}\ \Big] \]
$L_T$ — prior matchingNOT trained

Does the forward chain end where sampling begins? No parameters — both sides fixed. We enforce it via the schedule, not learn it. Detail in §6.

$L_{t-1}$ — denoising matchingTRAINED · the whole job

Match the model kernel $p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)$ to the true reverse step $q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)$, which is a tractable Gaussian (§7). This is the only place $\theta$ appears — everything downstream is this term simplified.

$L_0$ — reconstructiondecoder

The final step back to discrete (e.g. 8-bit) pixel data, handled by a dedicated decoder. A boundary term that folds into the $t=1$ case; it does not affect the core $\eps$-loss.

6 The term we only enforce

Why $L_T$ is a constraint, not a lesson

Look hard at $L_T=\KL\!\big(q(\x_T\mid\x_0)\,\|\,p(\x_T)\big)$. From §2, the left side is $q(\x_T\mid\x_0)=\N(\sqrt{\ab_T}\,\x_0,(1-\ab_T)\Id)$, fixed by the schedule; the right side is $p(\x_T)=\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$, fixed by us. No $\theta$ appears anywhere — gradient descent cannot touch it.

So what is it doing in the loss? It is a self-consistency requirement. Sampling starts by drawing $\x_T\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$; that is only sensible if the forward process genuinely delivers data to that distribution. As $\ab_T\to 0$ (guaranteed because each $\alpha_s\lt 1$ and they multiply), the mean $\sqrt{\ab_T}\,\x_0\to\mathbf 0$ and the covariance $(1-\ab_T)\Id\to\Id$, so $q(\x_T\mid\x_0)\to\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$ and $L_T\to 0$.

✓ The confirmation

$L_T$ tells us the forward chain must terminate in the same random distribution we sample from — and we do not learn this, we enforce it by choosing a schedule that drives $\ab_T\approx 0$. Carrying no parameters and being $\approx 0$ by construction, it is simply dropped from the training loss. A design constraint wearing the costume of a loss term.

7 The target the model chases

The true reverse step is a tractable Gaussian

Inside $L_{t-1}$ sits $q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)$ — the reverse step when we are allowed to peek at $\x_0$. Bayes' rule combines two Gaussians we already have; completing the square gives a Gaussian with a mean and variance we can read off explicitly. Here is the full computation.

  1. \[ q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0) = \frac{q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1})\,q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_0)}{q(\x_t\mid\x_0)} \ \propto\ q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1})\,q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_0) \]

    Bayes; the denominator is constant in $\x_{t-1}$, so drop it and restore normalization at the end. Both factors are known Gaussians ($q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1},\x_0)=q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1})$ by Markov).

  2. \[ \propto \exp\!\left(-\half\!\left[\frac{(\x_t-\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\x_{t-1})^2}{\beta_t} + \frac{(\x_{t-1}-\sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\x_0)^2}{1-\ab_{t-1}}\right]\right) \]

    Write out both Gaussian exponents. (Per-coordinate; the covariances are isotropic so we treat one dimension and the rest follow identically.)

  3. \[ = \exp\!\left(-\half\!\left[\Big(\tfrac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t}+\tfrac{1}{1-\ab_{t-1}}\Big)\x_{t-1}^2 -2\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t}\x_t + \tfrac{\sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}}{1-\ab_{t-1}}\x_0\Big)\x_{t-1} + C\Big)\right] \]

    Expand the squares and collect powers of $\x_{t-1}$. $C$ gathers terms independent of $\x_{t-1}$ (absorbed by normalization).

  4. \[ \frac{1}{\tilde\beta_t} := \frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t}+\frac{1}{1-\ab_{t-1}} = \frac{\alpha_t(1-\ab_{t-1})+\beta_t}{\beta_t(1-\ab_{t-1})} = \frac{1-\ab_t}{\beta_t(1-\ab_{t-1})} \]

    Variance. A Gaussian $\propto\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2\tilde\beta_t}(\x_{t-1}-\tilde\mu_t)^2)$ has precision $=$ the $\x_{t-1}^2$ coefficient. The numerator simplifies using $\beta_t=1-\alpha_t$ and $\alpha_t\ab_{t-1}=\ab_t$: $\ \alpha_t(1-\ab_{t-1})+\beta_t = \alpha_t-\ab_t+1-\alpha_t = 1-\ab_t$.

  5. \[ \fwd{\tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\ab_{t-1}}{1-\ab_t}\,\beta_t}, \qquad \tilde\mu_t = \tilde\beta_t\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t}\x_t + \frac{\sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}}{1-\ab_{t-1}}\x_0\right) \]

    Mean. For a Gaussian, $\tilde\mu_t = (\text{precision})^{-1}\times(\text{linear coefficient})$. Substitute the linear coefficient from step 3.

  6. \[ \rev{\tilde\mu_t(\x_t,\x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\ab_t}\,\x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\ab_{t-1})}{1-\ab_t}\,\x_t} \]

    Plug $\tilde\beta_t$ into step 5 and simplify each coefficient. This is DDPM Eq. (7).

\[ q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0) = \N\!\big(\x_{t-1};\ \rev{\tilde\mu_t(\x_t,\x_0)},\ \fwd{\tilde\beta_t}\,\Id\big). \]tractable posterior

This is the crux of the construction. The reverse step in general is not tractable — but conditioned on $\x_0$ it becomes an exact Gaussian. That is why $L_{t-1}$ is a KL between two Gaussians and not something hopeless. Our model $p_\theta$ sees only $\x_t$ (no $\x_0$), so its job is to reproduce this mean using $\x_t$ and $t$ alone.

8 A question that deserves care

Why is it legitimate to make $p_\theta$ Gaussian?

Here is the subtlety that makes this worth a whole section. In §7 we did prove a Gaussian — but it was $q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)$, conditioned on $\x_0$. At generation time the sampler never has $\x_0$. So there are two different reverse distributions, and it matters which one each part of the pipeline uses:

Training targetwhat the loss compares against

$L_{t-1}=\KL\big(q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)\big)$. The left argument is the $\x_0$-conditioned posterior — exactly Gaussian by §7.

Sampling targetwhat the trained model must stand in for

At generation the network must invert $q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)$ — the marginal reverse, with no $\x_0$. This one is generally non-Gaussian. Whether a Gaussian $p_\theta$ suffices is the real question.

Fact 1 — during training there is no approximation at all

Read the training target again. The loss $L_{t-1}$ asks $p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)$ to match $q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)$, and §7 showed that object is exactly $\N(\tilde\mu_t,\tilde\beta_t\Id)$. A Gaussian $p_\theta$ has precisely the right functional form to drive this KL to zero — we are matching a Gaussian with a Gaussian. So the Gaussian assumption costs nothing in the objective we actually optimize; there is no non-Gaussian target being crudely approximated. That is Fact 1, and it is airtight. The genuine question lives on the sampling side.

Fact 2 — at sampling time the marginal reverse is Gaussian, to $O(\beta_t^{3/2})$

The distribution the sampler must invert is, by Bayes with the true marginals $q(\x_{t-1})$ and $q(\x_t)$ (the data density pushed $t-1$ and $t$ steps forward),

\[ q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t) = \frac{q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1})\,q(\x_{t-1})}{q(\x_t)}. \]

Because $q(\x_{t-1})$ is multimodal, this is not Gaussian in general. The claim to prove (Feller 1949; used by Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015) is that it becomes Gaussian as $\beta_t\to 0$. We show it by a short-step (Laplace) expansion, keeping careful track of orders. Fix $\x_t$ and write $\u=\x_{t-1}-\x_t$.

  1. \[ \log q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t) = \log q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1}) + \log q(\x_{t-1}) + \text{const}(\x_{t-1}) \]

    Take logs of the Bayes expression. The $-\log q(\x_t)$ term is constant in $\x_{t-1}$, so it only fixes normalization.

  2. \[ \log q(\x_t\mid\x_{t-1}) = -\frac{\|\x_t-\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\x_{t-1}\|^2}{2\beta_t} = -\frac{\|\u\|^2}{2\beta_t} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\x_t^\top\u + O(\beta_t) \]

    The forward kernel concentrates $\x_{t-1}$. Substitute $\x_{t-1}=\x_t+\u$ and $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}=1-\tfrac{\beta_t}{2}+O(\beta_t^2)$, then expand. The dominant term $-\|\u\|^2/2\beta_t$ is an isotropic Gaussian in $\u$ of variance $\beta_t$, so all the mass sits within an $O(\sqrt{\beta_t})$ ball of $\x_t$: under $q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)$, typical $\u=O(\sqrt{\beta_t})$.

  3. \[ \log q(\x_{t-1}) = \log q(\x_t+\u) = \log q(\x_t) + \s^\top\u + \tfrac{1}{2}\u^\top H\u + \tfrac{1}{6}T[\u,\u,\u] + O(\|\u\|^4) \]

    Taylor-expand the smooth factor. Because $\u$ only ranges over that tiny $O(\sqrt{\beta_t})$ ball, we may expand the slowly-varying data marginal $q(\x_{t-1})$ around $\x_t$. Here $\s=\nabla\log q(\x_t)$ is the score, $H=\nabla^2\log q(\x_t)$ the Hessian, and $T$ the third-derivative tensor — all fixed properties of the data density, so all $O(1)$ in $\beta_t$.

  4. \[ \log q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t) = \underbrace{-\frac{\|\u\|^2}{2\beta_t}}_{O(1)} \;+\; \underbrace{\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\x_t+\s\big)^{\!\top}\!\u}_{O(\beta_t^{1/2})} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\u^\top H\u}_{O(\beta_t)} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{6}T[\u,\u,\u]}_{O(\beta_t^{3/2})} \;+\;\cdots \]

    Add and count orders at the typical scale $\u=O(\sqrt{\beta_t})$: the leading quadratic is $O(1)$; the linear term $O(\beta_t^{1/2})$; the Hessian's quadratic term $O(\beta_t)$; the cubic term $O(\beta_t^{3/2})$; and so on.

  5. \[ q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t) = \N\!\Big(\x_t+\beta_t\,\nabla\log q(\x_t),\ \ \beta_t\Id\Big)\cdot\Big(1+O(\beta_t^{3/2})\Big) \]

    Read it off. Every term through second order in $\u$ is quadratic in the exponent — and a quadratic exponent is exactly a Gaussian. Completing the square on the $O(1)$ and $O(\beta_t^{1/2})$ pieces gives mean $\x_t+\beta_t(\tfrac12\x_t+\s)\approx\x_t+\beta_t\nabla\log q(\x_t)$; the Hessian term perturbs the covariance to $\beta_t\Id+O(\beta_t^2)$. The first term a Gaussian cannot capture is the cubic, of size $O(\beta_t^{3/2})$. As $\beta_t\to 0$ it vanishes.

That is the proof, not a hand-wave: the reverse conditional's log-density is quadratic (hence Gaussian) up to a cubic correction that shrinks like $\beta_t^{3/2}$. Take a big step and $\beta_t$ (or the effective gap) is $O(1)$, the cubic and higher terms are no longer small, and the true conditional is genuinely multimodal — a single Gaussian then fits it badly. Keep the steps small and the Gaussian is asymptotically exact.

→ Two things fall out of this

(1) It forces many small steps. The Gaussian reverse model is justified only in the small-$\beta_t$ regime, which is exactly why sampling marches one small step at a time (§12) — and the constraint DDIM (§13) later renegotiates. (2) It reveals the score. The optimal Gaussian mean is $\x_t+\beta_t\,\nabla\log q(\x_t)$ — the current point nudged along the score of the data density. That is precisely what $\eps_\theta$ learns: from the marginal $\x_t=\sqrt{\ab_t}\x_0+\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\eps$ one has $\nabla\log q(\x_t)=-\eps/\sqrt{1-\ab_t}$ in expectation, so predicting $\eps$ (§10) and predicting the score are the same task up to scale — the bridge to score-based generative models.

9 KL collapses

A KL between two Gaussians is a mean-matching MSE

The standard simplification: do not learn the reverse variance. Fix $\Sigma_\theta=\sigma_t^2\Id$ to a schedule constant (DDPM uses $\sigma_t^2=\beta_t$ or $\tilde\beta_t$; either works). When two Gaussians share the same covariance, their KL is just the squared distance between their means. Full derivation from the general Gaussian KL:

  1. \[ \KL\!\big(\N(\mu_1,\Sigma_1)\,\|\,\N(\mu_2,\Sigma_2)\big) = \half\!\left[\log\frac{|\Sigma_2|}{|\Sigma_1|} - d + \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_2^{-1}\Sigma_1) + (\mu_1-\mu_2)^\top\Sigma_2^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2)\right] \]

    The general closed form for the KL between two $d$-dimensional Gaussians.

  2. \[ \Sigma_1=\Sigma_2=\Sigma \ \Rightarrow\ \log\frac{|\Sigma|}{|\Sigma|}=0,\quad \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma)=\operatorname{tr}(\Id)=d \ \Rightarrow\ \KL = \half(\mu_1-\mu_2)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2) \]

    Equal covariances kill the log-det ($=0$) and cancel the trace against $-d$. Only the Mahalanobis mean gap survives.

  3. \[ \Sigma=\sigma_t^2\Id \ \Rightarrow\ \KL = \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\|\mu_1-\mu_2\|^2 \]

    Isotropic covariance turns the quadratic form into a plain scaled squared norm.

Applying this to $L_{t-1}=\KL\!\big(q(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t)\big)$ with means $\tilde\mu_t$ and $\mu_\theta$:

\[ L_{t-1} = \E_q\!\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\big\|\,\rev{\tilde\mu_t(\x_t,\x_0)} - \rev{\mu_\theta(\x_t,t)}\,\big\|^2\right] + C. \]

The grand KL objective is now an ordinary regression: make the network's predicted mean land on the true posterior mean. $C$ collects $\theta$-independent constants.

10 The pivot to noise prediction

Reparameterize the mean — predict $\eps$, not $\mu$

We could ask the network to output $\mu_\theta$ directly, but a change of variables makes the target dramatically simpler. From the forward marginal (§2), invert to express $\x_0$ through the noise that produced $\x_t$: $\ \x_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\ab_t}}\big(\x_t - \sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps\big)$. Substitute into $\tilde\mu_t$.

  1. \[ \tilde\mu_t = \frac{\sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\ab_t}\cdot\frac{\x_t-\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps}{\sqrt{\ab_t}} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\ab_{t-1})}{1-\ab_t}\,\x_t \]

    Insert $\x_0$ into the posterior mean from §7, step 6.

  2. \[ \text{coeff. of }\x_t:\ \ \frac{\beta_t}{(1-\ab_t)\sqrt{\alpha_t}} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\ab_{t-1})}{1-\ab_t} = \frac{\beta_t + \alpha_t(1-\ab_{t-1})}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\ab_t)} = \frac{1-\ab_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\ab_t)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \]

    Use $\sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\ab_t}=1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$, then the same identity $\beta_t+\alpha_t(1-\ab_{t-1})=1-\ab_t$ from §7.

  3. \[ \text{coeff. of }\eps:\ \ -\frac{\beta_t}{(1-\ab_t)\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\cdot\sqrt{1-\ab_t} = -\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\sqrt{1-\ab_t}} \]

    Collect the $\eps$ term and simplify $\sqrt{1-\ab_t}/(1-\ab_t)=1/\sqrt{1-\ab_t}$.

  4. \[ \therefore\quad \tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(\x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\ab_t}}\,\eps\right) \]

    A strikingly tidy form: the only unknown on the right is the noise $\eps$ that generated $\x_t$.

So let the network predict that noise, $\eps_\theta(\x_t,t)$, and define $\mu_\theta$ to mirror the formula exactly:

\[ \rev{\mu_\theta(\x_t,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(\x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\ab_t}}\,\eps_\theta(\x_t,t)\right)}. \]

Now the mean difference is just a scaled noise difference, and the squared error follows immediately:

  1. \[ \tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta = \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\sqrt{1-\ab_t}}\big(\eps - \eps_\theta(\x_t,t)\big) \]

    The $\x_t$ terms are identical in both means and cancel; only the noise gap remains, times a constant.

  2. \[ L_{t-1} = \E_{\x_0,\eps}\!\left[\ \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\ab_t)}\ \big\|\,\eps - \eps_\theta(\underbrace{\sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0+\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps}_{=\,\x_t},\,t)\big\|^2\ \right] \]

    Square the constant and divide by $2\sigma_t^2$ (from §9). We generate $\x_t$ by the one-shot jump, so the whole term is an expectation over data $\x_0$ and a fresh noise $\eps$.

✓ The entire training loop, in one line

Sample $\x_0$ from data, sample $\eps\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id)$, form $\x_t=\sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0+\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps$, and train the network to recover the noise you added. The colored prefactor is a per-timestep weight — the subject of §11.

11 A modeling choice

Keep the weight, or drop it?

The weighted term above is the honest ELBO. DDPM famously observed that setting the weight to $1$ — training on plain $\|\eps-\eps_\theta\|^2$ — trains more stably and produces better samples. Both are defensible; they optimize different things.

\[ L_\text{simple} = \E_{t,\,\x_0,\,\eps}\Big[\ \big\|\,\eps - \eps_\theta(\sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0+\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps,\ t)\big\|^2\ \Big]. \]

Why dropping the weight is legitimate. For each fixed $t$, the weight $\lambda_t=\tfrac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\ab_t)}$ is a positive constant that does not depend on $\theta$. It therefore cannot move the minimizer of that timestep's term — the best $\eps_\theta$ is the same with or without it. Dropping $\lambda_t$ only changes how the timesteps are weighted relative to each other in the total sum: a reweighting of the gradient across $t$, not the target at any $t$.

Why you might prefer to drop it. The true $\lambda_t$ is large at small $t$ (easy, nearly-clean denoising) and small at large $t$ (hard, very-noisy denoising). Flattening it to $1$ up-weights the hard, high-noise steps — the ones that matter most for perceptual sample quality. A deliberate, beneficial reweighting.

\[ L_\text{vlb} = \E_{\x_0}\!\Big[\,L_0 + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\lambda_t\,\|\eps-\eps_\theta\|^2 + L_T\,\Big],\qquad \lambda_t=\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\ab_t)}. \]

Why keep the weight sometimes. Only the weighted sum equals the negative ELBO, a genuine bound on $-\log p_\theta(\x_0)$. If you care about likelihood — bits/dim, comparing density models, anything where the number must be a valid log-likelihood bound — you need the exact $\lambda_t$. Drop it and $L_\text{simple}$ is a fine training surrogate but no longer bounds anything. (Improved-DDPM keeps a learned variance and these weights precisely to recover competitive likelihoods.)

The one-line rule

Care about sample quality → drop the weight (use $L_\text{simple}$). Care about likelihood / a valid bound → keep it (use $L_\text{vlb}$). The optimum at each individual $t$ is identical either way; only the cross-$t$ balance changes.

12 The sampler's constraint

Why sampling marches $\x_t\to\x_{t-1}\to\x_{t-2}$, one small step at a time

Why crawl through a thousand steps at generation time? Why not learn one big map $p(\x_{t-k}\mid\x_t)$ and leap? Two linked reasons, both already in the math.

1 · A big reverse jump is not Gaussian

Our entire model class is Gaussian reverse kernels, and §8 showed that is accurate only for small steps. The true multi-step reverse $q(\x_{t-k}\mid\x_t)$ integrates over many possible clean images and is sharply multimodal — a single Gaussian cannot represent it. Forcing one to fit averages across the modes, and its mean lands in low-density space (blurry, mode-averaged samples).

2 · The forward jump needs $\x_0$, which the sampler lacks

Yes, we can jump forward in one shot (§2) — but that is $q(\x_t\mid\x_0)$, needing $\x_0$, the very thing we are trying to generate. Reversing requires marginalizing $\x_0$ out, which reintroduces the intractable multimodal integral. The small step is the price of not knowing $\x_0$.

σ The variance intuition, made precise

Each ancestral (DDPM) reverse step also injects fresh noise $\sigma_t\z$. Over many small steps this is fine; but cover the same ground in a few big stochastic jumps and the injected-noise variance per step dominates the signal correction — samples degrade. The way out is to remove the stochastic injection. That is precisely DDIM. →

13 Escaping the step count

DDIM: same training, deterministic sampling, big skips

The realization DDIM (Song et al., 2021) is built on: look back at $L_\text{simple}$ — it depends on $q$ only through the marginals $q(\x_t\mid\x_0)$. It never references the fact that the forward chain is Markov. So any forward process with the same marginals induces the same objective — and reuses the same trained $\eps_\theta$.

DDIM exploits this with a non-Markovian forward family, indexed by a free noise level $\sigma_t\ge 0$, defined by its posterior:

\[ q_\sigma(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0) = \N\!\left(\ \sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\x_0 + \sqrt{1-\ab_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\cdot\frac{\x_t - \sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0}{\sqrt{1-\ab_t}},\ \ \sigma_t^2\Id\ \right). \]

Proof that the marginals are preserved

The family is engineered so that $q_\sigma(\x_t\mid\x_0)=\N(\sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0,(1-\ab_t)\Id)$ for every $\sigma$ — identical to DDPM. We verify by induction (Song et al., Lemma 1): assume it holds at step $t$; propagate one step down.

  1. \[ q_\sigma(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_0) = \int q_\sigma(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_t,\x_0)\,q_\sigma(\x_t\mid\x_0)\,d\x_t \]

    Marginalize $\x_t$. Both factors are Gaussian and the mean of the first is affine in $\x_t$, so the result is Gaussian — we just need its mean and variance.

  2. \[ \E[\x_{t-1}] = \sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\x_0 + \sqrt{1-\ab_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\cdot\frac{\E[\x_t]-\sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0}{\sqrt{1-\ab_t}} = \sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\x_0 \]

    Mean. By hypothesis $\E[\x_t]=\sqrt{\ab_t}\,\x_0$, so the second term's numerator vanishes. Correct mean.

  3. \[ \operatorname{Var}[\x_{t-1}] = \Big(\underbrace{\tfrac{1-\ab_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}{1-\ab_t}}_{c^2}\Big)\underbrace{(1-\ab_t)}_{\operatorname{Var}[\x_t]} + \sigma_t^2 = (1-\ab_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2) + \sigma_t^2 = 1-\ab_{t-1} \]

    Variance. Propagate $\operatorname{Var}[\x_t]=1-\ab_t$ through the coefficient $c$, then add the kernel's own $\sigma_t^2$. The $\sigma_t^2$ terms cancel exactly — this is why the $\sqrt{1-\ab_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}$ coefficient is chosen precisely so.

  4. \[ \therefore\quad q_\sigma(\x_{t-1}\mid\x_0) = \N\!\big(\sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\x_0,\ (1-\ab_{t-1})\Id\big) \quad\checkmark \]

    Same form one step down — induction closes. The marginals are identical to DDPM for all $\sigma$, so the same $\eps_\theta$ trained under $L_\text{simple}$ is valid without retraining.

The sampling update

At inference we do not have $\x_0$, so we estimate it from the current noise prediction (invert the marginal), then step via $q_\sigma$:

\[ \good{\hat\x_0(\x_t,t)} = \frac{\x_t - \sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps_\theta(\x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\ab_t}}, \]
\[ \x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\good{\hat\x_0} + \sqrt{1-\ab_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\eps_\theta(\x_t,t) + \sigma_t\,\z,\qquad \z\sim\N(\mathbf 0,\Id). \]

The single knob $\sigma_t$ interpolates between two known samplers:

  • Set $\sigma_t = \sqrt{\tfrac{1-\ab_{t-1}}{1-\ab_t}}\sqrt{1-\tfrac{\ab_t}{\ab_{t-1}}} = \sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}$ and you recover ancestral DDPM sampling exactly — fully stochastic.
  • Set $\sigma_t = 0$ and the reverse step becomes fully deterministic — the $\sigma_t\z$ term vanishes. This is DDIM.

With $\sigma_t=0$, the update reads: predict a clean image, then re-noise it to the previous level using the same $\eps_\theta$, no random $\z$:

\[ \x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\ab_{t-1}}\,\good{\hat\x_0} + \sqrt{1-\ab_{t-1}}\;\eps_\theta(\x_t,t). \]

Why this lets you skip steps

Determinism turns sampling into solving an ordinary differential equation (the probability-flow ODE) rather than a stochastic one. There is no fresh noise accumulating, so the trajectory is smooth and a numerical solver can take large steps and still land near the exact solution. Concretely, pick any increasing sub-sequence $\tau_1\lt\dots\lt\tau_S$ of $\{1,\dots,T\}$ and apply the update between consecutive $\tau$'s (replacing the $t,t-1$ indices by $\tau_i,\tau_{i-1}$). Because the network is reused unchanged, you train once at $T=1000$ and sample in 20–50 steps.

? Yes — do we actually skip steps? (a common point of confusion)

Yes, and the original DDIM paper is where it comes from. Keep two separate ideas apart:

  • DDPM (Ho, Jain & Abbeel, 2020) runs the full chain — all $T=1000$ reverse steps, one at a time (ancestral sampling). It does not skip. That is why it is slow.
  • DDIM (Song, Meng & Ermon, ICLR 2021) introduces skipping, in its §4.2 “Accelerated Generation Processes.” Since the loss only depends on the marginals (proved just above), the sampler can be defined on any sub-sequence $\tau_1\lt\dots\lt\tau_S \subsetneq \{1,\dots,T\}$ and jump between those timesteps. Their experiments run $S \in \{10,20,50,100\}$ and report, e.g., CIFAR-10 FID $\approx 4.67$ at $50$ steps vs $4.04$ at $1000$ — essentially unchanged — while stochastic DDPM degrades sharply at the same low step counts.

Two distinct axes. Skipping (the sub-sequence $\tau$) is the actual speedup, and it is enabled by marginal-preservation. Determinism ($\sigma_t=0$) is what makes those big skips accurate. You can technically skip with stochastic sampling too, but the injected noise (§12) makes few-step stochastic sampling much worse — which is why DDIM pairs skipping with $\sigma_t=0$.

14 The honest accounting

How we got to DDIM, the speedup, and what it costs

Three pieces of one deliberate trade, laid side by side.

Why we ended up at DDIM

DDPM sampling is slow by construction: the Gaussian reverse kernel is only accurate over tiny steps (§8), and each stochastic step injects fresh noise $\sigma_t\z$. Try to cover the same ground in a few big stochastic jumps and that injected variance swamps the signal correction — samples fall apart (§12). The escape: the loss pins only the marginals $q(\x_t\mid\x_0)$, never the Markov joint — so we may swap in a different reverse process with the same marginals and the same $\eps_\theta$. That freedom is DDIM.

Why it speeds things upthe win

Setting $\sigma_t=0$ removes the injected noise, so sampling is a deterministic ODE instead of an SDE. An ODE trajectory is smooth — the same clean-image estimate $\hat\x_0$ is repeatedly refined, not re-randomized — so a coarse solver lands close to the exact solution. Big skips no longer accumulate injected-noise variance because there is none. That is the whole speedup: 10–50× fewer network evaluations for a comparable image.

Why it can cost qualitythe trade

Two distinct costs. (1) Discretization error: with very few steps you solve the ODE coarsely, so the trajectory only approximates the true path and fine detail degrades as steps drop (a 5-step image is visibly worse than a 50-step one). (2) Lost stochasticity: the injected DDPM noise is not only harmful — it also resamples, letting the chain correct small errors and explore the full data distribution. Removing it makes generation a deterministic map from $\x_T$ to one image, slightly reducing diversity and, when you can afford many steps, giving marginally worse fidelity than fully-stochastic DDPM. The honest rule: few steps → DDIM wins big; many steps → stochastic DDPM edges ahead.

σ The knob is continuous

Because $\sigma_t$ is a free parameter, DDIM and DDPM are the two ends of one dial, not rival algorithms. Turn $\sigma_t$ up toward $\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}$ to buy back diversity and high-step fidelity; turn it to $0$ to buy speed and reproducibility (a fixed $\x_T$ always yields the same image — useful for interpolation and editing). You choose where on the speed ↔ quality/diversity trade to sit.
